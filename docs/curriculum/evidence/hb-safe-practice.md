## Lesson hb-safe-practice-reflection

- Every step of PCI taught as goal, how, challenges, what can go wrong; each challenge and complication discussed as causes, prevention, treatment: brilakis p.26 (Introduction)
- The algorithms are the authors' and not necessarily the best; feedback invited; "always improving" so the best possible outcome is achieved for each patient: brilakis p.26 (Introduction)
- Results review: clinical indication, diagnostic information, admission information; every stored loop examined and complications such as dissection or embolism excluded; stenosis quantification reassessed; haemodynamic data examined; bloods (renal function, cholesterol, glucose); after complex PCI ECG and troponin; review sessions are educational and the whole team should be invited: oxford p.339
- Physiologist record: accurate recording of the whole procedure; printouts of baseline and final ECG and pressure tracings and of main events; date and start and end time; drugs and time of administration; contrast volume; equipment: oxford pp.61–62
- Team operational state: watching it can help identify conditions, such as excessive fatigue, that may lead to suboptimal outcomes: brilakis p.39 (2.9). Avoid noise and distraction; sterile cockpit rule in critical parts: brilakis p.39 (2.10)
- "Was the operator post-call? Was the room noisy? Was the right equipment there? Was the case squeezed in?": this module's reasoning from brilakis p.39 and p.146 (7.3.1 site of service lacking equipment, scheduling challenges)
- Outcome bias, replay loops and compartmentalisation listed as cognitive and cultural barriers to an operator's recovery after intra-procedural death: ref touma-operator-grief-2025 (abstract). "Outcome bias distorts case reviews in the same way", "grade the decision and the outcome separately": this module's reasoning (the paper does not discuss case review)
- Leaders make sure systems exist so that lessons are learnt from adverse incidents and near misses, shared with the team, concrete action follows, practice changed where needed: ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 para 32c
- Take part in regular reviews and audits of the team's standards and performance; discuss adverse incidents and near misses at appraisal: ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 para 29
- Example action ("add the dissection bail-out kit to the CTO trolley checklist..."): this module's illustration
- M&M a double-edged sword; successful, it models learning from error; unsuccessful, conveys that learning from error is unproductive at best and unpleasant at worst; attention to how it is conducted: ref orlander-mm-conference-2002 (abstract)
- Cardiologist's duty to debrief the lab team at the end of the day, especially after complications or when important learning points should be shared: oxford p.48
- Report at an early stage so lessons are learnt quickly: ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 para 23. Must not try to prevent colleagues raising concerns: para 28. Senior clinicians set an example of openness and foster a culture of learning and improvement: para 30
- Presenting rules (facts first, decision points, manual as standard, system factors, actions with owners): this module's reasoning from the frames above; confidentiality: Good medical practice para 73 as quoted in ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 Appendix 1 ("When providing information for these purposes you must follow our guidance on Confidentiality")
- Reflective capacity regarded by many as essential to professional competence; evidence for reflection curricula largely theoretical; both stated in the abstract as the background that motivated the review, and the module presents them that way: ref mann-reflection-review-2009 (abstract)
- Check 1: causes/prevention/treatment frame (brilakis p.26); preparation of calcified lesions listed under prevention of dissection: brilakis p.438 (25.2.1); B("25.2") added to the lesson sources
- Check 2: ref orlander-mm-conference-2002; ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 paras 23–28 (open reporting supported by the organisation)
- Tip pci-safe-capture-the-facts: physiologist's timed log (oxford pp.61–62); causes/prevention/treatment, the frame Brilakis applies to every complication (brilakis p.26, Introduction, named in the tip text; applied to acute closure in 25.1–25.2, p.435 onwards; sources 25.1 and 25.2)
- Tip diag-operator-review-the-runs: existing tip (hb-the-operator); oxford p.339
- Pearl (one-page template; recurring system factor becomes an audit topic): this module's reasoning
- Videos: titles only ("When disaster strikes...", "Complex Coronary Interventions webcast XXVI", "CCI webcast XXVII"; registry src/content/videos.ts)

## Lesson hb-safe-practice-audit

- Accredited centre: independent unit; more than 800 coronary interventions a year; electronic database for diagnostic and interventional procedures regularly audited by the National Interventional Society; at least two supervisors with more than 1000 PCIs and over 5 years' experience: oxford p.50
- Radiographer: "to audit as necessary": oxford p.63. IR(ME)R: doses should be audited: oxford p.36
- Greater operator experience associated with higher success and lower complication rates; operator competence part of benefit-risk assessment: brilakis p.140 (7.1.8)
- Quality assessed through structure, process and outcome; assessment needs information about the causal linkages among them: ref donabedian-quality-1988 (abstract)
- Process examples: appropriate use criteria for stable CAD (brilakis p.29, 1.1); consent documented (brilakis p.29; oxford p.41); clinical evaluation recorded for every exposure (oxford p.36); record patient radiation and contrast doses (oxford p.63); stop before contrast volume of 3.7 × GFR, a lower threshold preferable in chronic kidney disease or single kidney, recent contrast taken into consideration (brilakis p.39, 2.6); CTO limit given as 3.7 × estimated creatinine clearance (brilakis p.394, 21.8.5); patients with more than 5 Gy air kerma followed up within a month with skin examination documented; for more than 10 Gy a qualified physicist promptly calculates peak skin dose and the skin is examined in 2–4 weeks (brilakis p.493, 28.2.3)
- Outcome examples and diagnostic catheterisation rates (death 0.1%, MI 0.1%, pulmonary oedema 0.1%, stroke 0.1%, tamponade 0.1%, contrast reaction 0.4%, vasovagal 2–3%, arrhythmia 0.4%, vascular complications 1–2%, coronary dissection 0.1%, air embolism 0.4%, renal failure 0.7%, cholesterol embolisation 1.4%): oxford p.299
- "Process measures frequent and within your control; outcomes rare and case-mix dependent": this module's reasoning
- 2020 ESC-ACVC QIs measured in 12,660/13,130 patients from FAST-MI 2005, 2010, 2015; most measurable from registries but not the patient-satisfaction domain; both composite QIs associated with adjusted 1-year mortality; centre benchmarking vs national mean feasible: ref schiele-acvc-qi-2021 (abstract)
- BCIS: all UK PCI hospitals; 1994 to present; about 80,000 new procedures a year; 113 variables; outcomes to discharge; live online analysis, monthly and quarterly reports incl. process measures and risk-adjusted outcome; annual national reports; PCI attempt definition; data entered locally by caregivers and data clerks; local validation, range and consistency checks; no external validation; feedback of completeness: ref ludman-bcis-registry-2011 (abstract)
- PROGRESS CTO 2018 update: 3,122 CTO PCIs, 20 centres; technical success 87%; in-hospital major complications 3.0%; median procedure time 123 min (quiz q10): ref tajti-progress-hybrid-2018 (note of that verified reference)
- "Benchmarks for experienced centres, not targets for a first-year CTO operator": this module's reasoning
- Check 1: ref ludman-bcis-registry-2011
- Steps 1–6 (denominator, definitions, prospective collection, small numbers, case mix, plot not rank): this module's reasoning built on ludman (attempt definition, risk-adjusted outcome) and spiegelhalter (funnel plots). Step 3 logbook items (role, access, presentation, complexity, techniques, complications): oxford p.50 (logbook contents; taught in hb-the-operator). Step 5 case-mix variables: risks increase with patient age and co-morbidity (oxford p.299); mortality risk at catheterisation increases with age, left main stenosis, three-vessel disease, impaired ventricular function, class IV heart failure, aortic valve disease (oxford p.300)
- Step 7: leaders make sure systems including auditing and benchmarking monitor, review and improve the team's work; work with others to collect and share information on patient experience and outcomes: ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 para 32, 32a. Those with a management role must make sure systems give early warning of failure in the clinical performance of individuals or teams, including audits and patient feedback: para 31
- Formula: arithmetic on oxford p.299 rates (400 × 1–2% = 4–8; 400 × 0.1% = 0.4): this module's reasoning. Oxford p.299 gives no access route for the 1–2%. MATRIX (ACS, radial vs femoral): radial reduced BARC major bleeding unrelated to CABG, 1.6% vs 2.3%: ref matrix-2015 (PMID 25791214, abstract checked). "A radial-first practice needs a benchmark built for it" / "may overestimate the expected rate" (q3): this module's reasoning from those two facts
- Funnel plot: estimate plotted against a measure of its precision; control limits form a funnel around the target; analogy with Shewhart control charts; handles over-dispersion from unmeasured risk factors; avoids spurious league tables: ref spiegelhalter-funnel-plots-2005 (abstract). "Usually the number of cases", "narrow on the right, wide on the left", "a result outside the limits is the signal to investigate; a result inside is consistent with the target for that number of cases": this module's explanation of the method (the abstract does not say that results inside the limits never merit attention, and the module does not say so)
- Public reporting: Medicare AMI patients 2002–2010; 3 reporting vs 7 regional non-reporting states; in 2010 less PCI in reporting states, largest differences in STEMI and cardiogenic shock or cardiac arrest; no difference in overall AMI mortality: ref joynt-public-reporting-2012 (abstract)
- Check 2: refs spiegelhalter-funnel-plots-2005, ludman-bcis-registry-2011, joynt-public-reporting-2012
- Tip pci-safe-count-every-attempt: ref ludman-bcis-registry-2011 (attempt definition); logbook (oxford p.50). Trigger narrowed to the finish phase after wire-not-crossing, balloon-uncrossable or stent-uncrossable, so it does not duplicate pci-operator-log-the-case at every finish
- Pearl (audit processes monthly, outcomes yearly): this module's reasoning
- Videos: titles only (PROGRESS CTO registry introduction; April 2017 update; May 2018 update)

## Lesson hb-safe-practice-medicolegal

- Only absolute contraindication to cardiac catheterisation is refusal of patient consent; relative contraindications listed: oxford p.25
- Consent table row 'What the discussion covers': death about 1 in 1000 at cardiac catheterisation; standard consent should mention mortality; mortality risk increases with age, left main stenosis, three-vessel disease, impaired ventricular function, class IV heart failure, aortic valve disease: oxford p.300
- Montgomery: material risks and reasonable alternatives: ref montgomery-2015 (as taught in bed-ind-decisions-consent). GMC 2020: consent as a dialogue: ref gmc-consent-2020 (as in bed-ind-decisions-consent)
- Consent obtained and documented prior to the procedure; discussion of risks and benefits of ad hoc PCI critical in patients without a prior angiogram: brilakis p.29 (1.1)
- Table 1.1 informed consent: obtained within 30 days; documented in the medical chart; healthcare proxy; DNR or DNI, or revoked for procedure: brilakis p.31 (1.1, Table 1.1; read on the PDF page)
- After appropriate discussion of risks and benefits, consent obtained and documented according to local guidelines: oxford p.41
- Consent form checked and signature confirmed with the patient; review notes to ensure the investigation is still clinically indicated: oxford p.43
- Sign in before sedation, GA or draping: identity (date of birth, hospital number), correct procedure, consent form: oxford p.55
- Ad hoc PCI disadvantages include less time for detailed discussion of risks and benefits: brilakis p.145 (7.3.1). Inadequate informed consent (diagnostic angiography identifies anatomy with PCI risk significantly higher than discussed) favours delayed PCI; complex or high-risk disease (unprotected left main, CTO, multivessel, bifurcations) favours delayed PCI: brilakis pp.145–146 (7.3.1)
- Check 1: brilakis pp.145–146; "a sedated patient on the table cannot weigh new material risks properly" and "no note written afterwards repairs a missing discussion": this module's reasoning from ref montgomery-2015 / gmc-consent-2020 as taught in bed-ind-decisions-consent
- Cardiologist report contents (history, indication, anatomy, steps, equipment with number, type, size and length of stents, complications and management, post-procedural plan): oxford p.48
- Nurse: clear and thorough documentation on the hospital's reporting platform; notes at the time of events, ideally by a second circulating nurse; patient's advocate: oxford p.54
- Physiologist record (whole procedure; baseline and final ECG and pressure; main events; start and end time; drugs and times; contrast; equipment; timing of wiring and of balloon/stent inflation; pressure and duration of each inflation; FFR/iFR values; timing and values of ACT checks; device used for arterial haemostasis; total procedural time; fluoroscopy time and dose area product; timing and management of intra-procedural complications; settings of temporary pacing, counterpulsation or other support devices): oxford pp.61–62
- Radiographer: record patient radiation and contrast doses per local policy; archive images to standards of exchanging, forwarding and storing digital data: oxford p.63
- Sign out by scrub nurse: confirm accurate documentation; equipment check, sharps count, wire integrity; record equipment left in situ; record unexpected issues: oxford pp.55–56
- After death: a readable written account from everyone involved (Oxford: all staff write legible notes), with exact timing of events, personnel present, equipment used, perceived mechanism of death; note whether next of kin contacted and what was said: oxford p.300 (reworded in round 2 to remove the source's sentence structure). "The same standard is a good one after any serious complication": this module's reasoning
- Tip pci-safe-scribe-the-timeline: oxford p.54 (second circulating nurse), p.300 (timed notes)
- Step 2: report includes complications and management: oxford p.48
- Step 3: professional duty (tell, apologise, offer remedy or support, explain short- and long-term effects): ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 (opening section); record apology details in clinical record: para 17f; apology does not mean admitting legal liability; Compensation Act 2006 (England and Wales) s2; NHS Resolution will never refuse cover on a claim because an apology has been given: para 15 and footnotes 13–14. Patients expect an apology to cover what happened, what can be done to deal with any harm, what will be done to prevent someone else being harmed: para 14 (also taught in hb-after-the-procedure-communication). Cardiologist's duty to inform patient and family of incidents or complications in line with duty of candour: oxford p.48
- Step 4: report early so lessons are learnt quickly; follow the organisation's policy: paras 23–24; the guidance names the NRLS or its replacement, LFPSE: para 25a and footnote 30; LFPSE has fully replaced the NRLS, decommissioned on 30 June 2024 (England): ref nhse-nrls-lfpse-transition-2026; in Wales all patient safety incidents should be reported through Datix Cymru (part of the Once for Wales Concerns Management System): ref nhs-wales-incident-policy-2023 (policy section 11.4 and definitions; covering circular WHC/2023/017); Yellow Card scheme to tell the MHRA about serious suspected adverse reactions to a medicine: para 25b ("contrast included": this module's application, contrast media being medicines); medical device incidents to the device safety lead (if any) and national body (MHRA in England and Wales): para 25c (ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015). After a death in the lab, speak to unit manager and complete an adverse incident form per local policy if there has been an iatrogenic complication: oxford p.300 (section on relatives after unexpected death)
- Step 5 (family): contact next of kin and/or family and explain the course of events; let the family view the body with suitable privacy if they wish; be aware of religious requirements; document whether next of kin contacted and what was said: oxford p.300
- Step 6 (who must be told): inform and discuss with the department manager and the coroner in case of intra-procedural death (listed among the cardiologist's duties): oxford p.48. Primary care physician notified early (involved in bereavement counselling); case discussed with the coroner; post-mortem requested if death unexplained (Oxford does not say who does these): oxford p.300. Notification of Deaths Regulations 2019: registered medical practitioner must notify the relevant senior coroner, as soon as reasonably practicable, in writing, when the practitioner suspects the death was due to a treatment or procedure of a medical or similar nature or (attending practitioner) the cause is unknown; in force 1 October 2019; England and Wales: ref notification-of-deaths-2019. Senior coroner may request a post-mortem examination: ref coroners-justice-act-2009 (s14(1)). "Other UK nations have their own arrangements; follow national and local procedure": this module's caution, no claim made about them
- Step 6: regulation 20 of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014; all providers registered with the CQC; open and transparent with people using services and families; notifiable safety incident: inform, reasonable support, truthful information, timely apology; CQC can prosecute breach of parts 20(2)(a) and 20(3); organisational duty does not apply to individuals; Scotland: Health (Tobacco, Nicotine etc. and Care) (Scotland) Act 2016 and Duty of Candour Procedure (Scotland) Regulations 2018, in effect 1 April 2018: ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 Appendix 2. Wales: Health and Social Care (Quality and Engagement) (Wales) Act, law on 1 June 2020, in force 1 April 2023, organisational duty of candour on providers of NHS services (NHS bodies and primary care): ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 Appendix 2 (which misprints the year as 2000); Act title and year checked on legislation.gov.uk: ref wales-candour-act-2020. Northern Ireland: consultation launched April 2021 on statutory duty proposals; no statutory duty described: ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 Appendix 2. (Step 6 of the earlier draft is now step 7.) Also checked on legislation.gov.uk (regulation 20 as made and as amended by SI 2015/64: applies to registered persons; notify as soon as reasonably practicable; in person; account of facts, further enquiries, apology; written follow-up; secure written record). "Your report is how the organisation learns the duty has been triggered": this module's reasoning
- Tip pci-post-complication-candour: existing tip (hb-after-the-procedure)
- Check 2: ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 paras 15, 23–24, Appendix 2
- IR(ME)R: register of referrers, practitioners, operators; cardiologist often referrer, practitioner and operator; clinical evaluation recorded for all exposures including fluoroscopy; doses audited; hospitals investigated regularly; dose greater than expected → local radiation protection adviser; overexposure: plaintiffs could bring an action under criminal (not civil) law: oxford p.36. Medical physics expert analyses accidental or unintended exposures (reg 14(3)(f)); enforced as health and safety regulations, offence provisions apply (reg 18): ref irmer-2017 note. Enforcing authority in England the CQC: IR(ME)R reg 2(1), checked on legislation.gov.uk. "Oxford's wording overstates this; prosecution is separate from any civil negligence claim a harmed patient may bring": this module's reading, supported by ref gmc-nmc-candour-2015 footnote 13 (legal liability means a clinical negligence claim)
- Incident definition (unintended exposure or greater than intended); near miss; incident not requiring notification; incident requiring notification; overexposure typically at least ten times the intended dose; radiographer warns at 1 Gy; operator records dose in report and communicates to patient: oxford p.39
- Significant exposure investigated immediately and notified to the enforcing authority; referrer, practitioner and patient informed of a clinically significant unintended or accidental exposure (reg 8): ref irmer-2017 note
- 1 Gy item additions: lab protocol for alerting the operator on radiation dose, e.g. announcing the dose every 1 Gray and/or every 30 min; consider stopping if 7–8 Gy air kerma and consider staging: brilakis p.492 (28.2.2.4.8). ## Lesson hb-safe-practice-wellbeing

- Operator and staff condition (well rested, not sleep deprived) can affect outcome: brilakis p.140 (7.1.8); team operational state, excessive fatigue: brilakis p.39 (2.9)
- Operator or patient fatigue after diagnostic catheterisation a reason for delayed rather than ad hoc PCI: brilakis p.146 (7.3.1); operator fatigue and patient fatigue favour staged PCI: brilakis p.146 (7.3.2); patient or physician fatigue a reason to stop a CTO attempt: brilakis p.394 (21.8.5)
- Survey: January 2023, 1,159 attendings and 192 fellows; selection bias; mean 63 ± 14 h (fellows 67 ± 14); 69% burnout impacted life; 64% emotionally exhausted; 78% working too hard; 41% considered leaving in the past year, 32% now; 84% lonely; 28% unhappy, single 54% vs partner 25%; call ≥3/week 74% vs 66%; contributors: excessive documentation, lack of administrative support, bureaucratic tasks, insufficient income (fellows' top contributor); leading coping: talking with family/friends; 67% someone to share concerns with; 37% employer mental health access; 76% not under or seeking professional care; main proposed solutions better administrative support (86%) and removal of rules/regulations that do not contribute to patient care (84%); fellows 48% lonely, 29% considered leaving in the past year, 15% now; stigma associated with mental disorders and fear of professional consequences suggested as reasons physicians avoid mental health care; employers may provide anonymised access to mental health professionals: ref simsek-wellbeing-survey-2023 (PMC full text, re-read for round 1). "Led from Minneapolis": author affiliations (Minneapolis Heart Institute)
- Lobo survey: 6,683 invited, 481 (7.2%); 70% interventional cardiologists; 91% call; 43% ≥7 call nights/month; sleep disorders 25%; sleep-inducing medication 25%; difficulty concentrating 58%, lack of motivation 56%, irritability 68%; 46% performance hindered; 8.6% adverse event likely related; 56.5% burnout; 85% favour policies to go home early post call: ref lobo-sleep-survey-2019 (abstract)
- Iverson: 12,680 daytime PCIs 6/2009–12/2016; 367 (2.9%) by sleep-deprived operators (daytime procedure preceded by a procedure between 12 am and 6:59 am on the same date); in-hospital death 1.1% vs 1.3%; bleeding within 72 h 3.9% vs 2.9%; not significant; single centre: ref iverson-sleep-pci-outcomes-2018 (abstract)
- Sandoval review: impaired performance, cognitive deficits, reduced psychomotor vigilance, workplace errors and injuries; paucity of IC data: ref sandoval-sleep-deprivation-2018 (abstract)
- Check 1: refs iverson, lobo; brilakis pp.146, 394
- Klein: 2014 SCAI survey, 314 responses; 49.4% at least one orthopaedic injury; cervical 24.7%, lumbar 34.4%, hip/knee/ankle 19.6%; age most significantly correlated; annual caseload associated; 6.9% limited caseload because of radiation; 9.3% health-related absence; consistent with 2004 survey, persisting despite increased awareness; radiation-associated problems (cataracts, cancers) asked about; the lesson quotes the abstract's phrase "a small but substantial incidence" with attribution and states that the abstract gives no figure and no comparison group: ref klein-occupational-hazards-2015 (abstract, re-checked on PubMed in round 2)
- Table as high as possible while comfortable and ergonomic for the operator; image receptor close to patient; operator far from patient and tube; shielding between patient and operator: brilakis p.125 (6.4.2)
- Lead gowns one-piece or separate top and skirt, the latter may suit those with back problems; most 0.25–0.35 mm LE; higher LE heavier: oxford p.36
- Ceiling-suspended lead protects the operator and obviates wearing lead and the associated orthopaedic injuries: brilakis p.489 (28.2.2.2.3)
- Tip diag-safe-ergonomic-setup: brilakis pp.125, 489; oxford p.36
- Death in the lab: one of the most traumatic complications; often a prolonged resuscitation, many staff; physically and psychologically demanding; cardiologist in charge ensures staff have time to talk and, if necessary, cancels or postpones further procedures; some hospitals offer counselling: oxford p.300
- Wu's 2000 BMJ editorial describes the clinician who makes a mistake as a 'second victim' who needs help too: ref wu-second-victim-2000 (title only; no claim that Wu coined the term)
- Grief, self-doubt, psychological trauma; systemic silence and lack of institutional support prolong distress; strategies reflective practice, reconnecting with patient families, peer dialogue, structured debriefing, culturally attuned support; vulnerability as professionalism, psychological safety: ref touma-operator-grief-2025 (abstract)

## Lesson hb-safe-practice-mindset

- Acute vessel closure can lead to significant patient and operator stress, which may lead to inadvertent removal of the guidewire; losing guidewire position can be catastrophic especially with dissection; guide manipulation can cause loss of wire position; remaining calm and maintaining true-lumen wire position is key: brilakis p.435 (25.1)
- Stoic video exists on the Brilakis channel ("How the Stoic Philosophy Can Help with CTO/complex PCI"): src/content/videos.ts (title only)
- Stoicism an ancient Greek and Roman philosophy; a way to process negative feelings while maintaining human connection; acceptance of what one cannot control; controlling emotions is not suppressing them but keeping them appropriate; widely misrepresented in medicine as a 'stiff upper lip' promoting detachment and suppression; Stoic ideals underpin CBT, though CBT has since evolved independently; practices: negative visualisation, Stoic mindfulness, Stoic reflection; 24 third-year medical students, 12 days online (SeRenE); Stoic ideation, resilience and empathy increased; no significant correlation between Stoic ideation change and resilience or empathy change; small sample, no control group, self-selection; negative visualisation aided emotional and practical preparedness, contingency plans, less catastrophising; Stoic mindfulness: thinking about how one thinks and feels: ref brown-stoic-training-2022 (PMC full text)
- Stoicism (upper case) promotes resilience, self-control and rational acceptance of adversity; stoic attitudes / pseudo-stoicism (emotional suppression, silent endurance) linked in cancer patients to reduced social support, delayed help-seeking, poorer psychological outcomes; stoic traits sometimes associated with persistence and treatment adherence: ref harerimana-stoic-attitudes-2025 (abstract). Every place the module states the suppression link (lesson list, check 1, q9, keypoint, case s6) names the cancer-patient population

- Table row 2: brilakis p.435 (25.1)
- Table row 3: brief covers each team member's role in case of complications or cardiac arrest: oxford p.55 (and cardiologist leads the brief, p.47); team should understand routines before commencing, e.g. who would defibrillate in VF: oxford p.43
- Table row 4: brilakis p.26; lesson hb-safe-practice-reflection
