## Lesson hb-lab-and-radiation-history

- Techniques learnt mainly by apprenticeship, guided by the trainer's skills and beliefs; value of an independent reference: oxford p.23
- Definition of cardiac catheterisation (catheter into left and/or right heart for diagnostic information about heart and/or vessels): oxford p.23
- Definition of coronary angiography (contrast into coronaries under X-ray, anatomy and degree of luminal obstruction; standard investigation for known or suspected CAD): oxford p.23
- Round 1: 'reference standard for coronary anatomy' paired with the ESC note that suspected CCS usually starts with a non-invasive test: ref esc-ccs-2024 (as taught in fnd-path-ccs, foundations.ts table of likelihood bands); 'Oxford calls it the standard investigation': oxford p.23
- Angiography limited to the lumen, 'lumenography'; gives information on atheroma extent, thrombus, spasm, myocardial bridging, dissection: oxford p.27
- PTCA evolved into PCI, covering balloon dilatation, stents and adjunctive therapeutic and imaging techniques: oxford p.205
- Forssmann 1929: modified urinary catheter; work first on cadavers; self-catheterisation with fluoroscopic control and a mirror; chest X-ray documented the first right heart catheterisation: oxford p.24
- Oxford route: internal jugular vein into right atrium: oxford p.24
- Alternative route: urethral catheter via his antecubital vein into his right ventricular cavity: ref packy-forssmann-2016 (abstract)
- 25-year-old surgical trainee, one year out of medical school; later visualised right heart chambers with iodinated contrast through a catheter, on himself: ref meyer-forssmann-1990 (abstract)
- Cournand 1941, catheterised RV, more detailed right heart studies: oxford p.24. Oxford's further reading on the same page lists Cournand's 1941 paper titled 'Catheterization of the right auricle in man' (Proc Soc Exp Biol Med 1941;46:462-66): oxford p.24; stated as a discrepancy
- Zimmerman 1947, first simultaneous left and right heart catheterisation: oxford p.24
- Zimmerman, Scott, Becker, 'Catheterization of the left side of the heart in man', Circulation, March 1950: ref zimmerman-left-heart-1950
- Seldinger 1953, Swedish radiologist, designed for arteriography; used today 'in modified form': oxford p.65. Original: needle, wire through it, needle removed, catheter passed over the wire, as the title 'Catheter replacement of the needle in percutaneous arteriography' states: ref seldinger-technique-1953 (title checked on PubMed, PMID 13057644; no abstract) and oxford p.66 (further reading)
- Modern form (hollow bevelled needle, atraumatic curved-tip wire through the needle, needle removed, introducer sheath with or without dilator over the wire, wire removed, haemostatic valve): oxford p.66 (Fig. 3.1)
- Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1956 to Forssmann, Cournand and Dickinson Richards: oxford p.24; ref packy-forssmann-2016
- Sones, Cleveland Clinic, selective coronary angiography, 1959: oxford p.24
- Sones's selective hand injection into the RCA of a middle-aged man on 30 October 1958: ref ryan-angiogram-history-2002 (abstract)
- Dotter and Judkins mid-1960s, sequentially larger coaxial sheaths to dilate leg arteries in severe peripheral rest ischaemia: oxford p.205
- Favaloro introduced aortocoronary bypass surgery in May 1967: ref ryan-angiogram-history-2002
- First PTCA in a conscious human, Zurich, 16 September 1977, Gruentzig: oxford p.205 (oxford p.24 gives 1977, Zurich)
- Preceding years developing a balloon for intracoronary inflation; animals, post-mortem specimens, living humans; first peripheral, then coronary with a miniaturised balloon during bypass surgery: oxford p.205
- Radial rather than longitudinal force as the key realisation: oxford p.205
- First PTCA patient: exertional angina, severe proximal LAD lesion; balloon crossed without event; inflated twice to relieve the trans-lesion gradient; no chest pain, ST shift or arrhythmia; good angiographic result: oxford p.205
- Age 38 (oxford p.24) versus 37-year-old insurance salesman (oxford p.205): internal disagreement in the handbook, stated as such
- Gruentzig's Lancet report 1978: ref gruntzig-ptca-1978; oxford p.24 (further reading)
- Rentrop July 1979: guidewire recanalisation, then intracoronary streptokinase: ref ryan-angiogram-history-2002
- First coronary stents implanted 1986: oxford p.24
- Palmaz and Schatz pioneered stents to prevent abrupt closure and, in particular, to scaffold balloon-induced dissection; near 100% uptake 'has paralleled' the fall in urgent CABG and procedural mortality <1% (a temporal parallel, worded as such): oxford p.205. The 1986 date is from oxford p.24 and is kept separate from the Palmaz-Schatz credit
- Rapid development of PCI procedures and devices since the early 1990s: oxford p.24
- Sigwart 1987: self-expanding stainless-steel mesh; 24 coronary stents in 19 patients with restenosis, abrupt closure after angioplasty or graft deterioration: ref sigwart-stents-1987 (abstract). Worded "an early human series", not "the first".
- Gruentzig's criteria (stable angina, documented ischaemia, single-vessel disease preferably proximal non-occluded non-calcified, no features precluding CABG such as malignancy, severe LV dysfunction, pulmonary disease); such patients still do well with low complication risk; today's subsets (primary PCI, ACS, cardiogenic shock, multivessel, bifurcation, LMS, vein graft, unfit for CABG): oxford p.207
- Pearl (every failed criterion is a reason to plan more): this module's reasoning from oxford p.207
- Pitfall (wall and plaque need intravascular imaging): lumen-only limitation oxford p.27; intravascular imaging as the tool for the wall is the module's link to pm-imaging, not a claim from these pages
- No video is listed for this topic in the brief; the lesson has none

## Lesson hb-lab-and-radiation-ct

- Invasive angiography the technique of choice owing to good spatial and good time resolution: oxford p.29
- CT acquisition of up to 320 slices in one rotation, whole heart in one short breath-hold: oxford p.29
- When the book was written, European and American guidelines recommended functional tests (cardiac MR, stress echo, SPECT, PET) first; CT-FFR and perfusion CT likely to change this: oxford p.29
- High sensitivity and NPV (up to 100%); specificity and PPV significantly lower (<50% in some studies), influenced by technical and patient factors: oxford p.29
- Accuracy influenced by scanner type (64-detector row minimum), pretest probability, high calcium score, high heart rate, high BMI; CT mainly to rule out: oxford p.29
- CT indications (typical/atypical angina or non-anginal pain with abnormal resting ECG; low-to-intermediate probability or inconclusive functional test; suspected ACS with inconclusive ECG and markers; SVG patency because less mobile, larger, tend not to calcify; CTO anatomy, distal landing zone and proximal cap; anomalous origin/anatomy including AV fistula): oxford p.29–30
- CT should not usually be considered: high pretest probability; calcium score >1000; large BMI; previous stenting (accuracy for ISR decreases with stents <3 mm and thick-strut first-generation): oxford p.30
- TAVI CT items (femoral/axillary size, tortuosity, disease for access selection; annulus diameter, area, perimeter for valve size and type; root and STJ relevant for self-expandable valves; whole aorta tortuosity, atherosclerosis, thrombus stratification, ulceration; valve calcification; coronary ostial height; angles for ideal angiographic working view): oxford p.30. Oxford gives a purpose only for rows 1-3 and row 7; rows 4-6 are marked 'no specific purpose given; part of deciding on and planning the procedure' from Oxford's lead-in ('a key step in the decision-making and planning')
- 2024 ESC CCS guideline: clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD guides the first test; CCTA preferred at low to moderate likelihood, functional imaging as likelihood rises, ICA when likelihood very high or high-risk features: ref esc-ccs-2024 (as summarised in the Academy lesson fnd-path-ccs)
- Round 1, ESC 2024 text checked on the OUP full text (Eur Heart J 2024;45(36):3415-3537): s3.3.1.3, in older patients with heavily calcified coronary arteries functional testing may be more appropriate than CCTA; s3.3.3.1, ICA indicated with a very high (>85%) likelihood, particularly with severe symptoms refractory to antianginal treatment or angina/dyspnoea at a low level of exercise; s3.3.4, going directly to ICA a reasonable option with symptoms unresponsive to therapy or low-exercise angina and high event risk: ref esc-ccs-2024
- Recent CTA allows target lesion determination before the procedure: brilakis p.33 (1.1)
- CT chest: anomalous arch; size and disease of iliac/subclavian vessels; arteria lusoria (anomalous origin of right subclavian from the arch) favours left radial or femoral access; CT abdomen/pelvis: CFA bifurcation and iliofemoral disease: brilakis p.34 (1.4)
- Aortic CT angiography: aortic dilation, anomalous coronary arteries: brilakis p.34 (1.5)
- Recent contrast (for example contrast CT) considered when setting the contrast threshold: brilakis p.39 (2.6)
- Careful planning prevents unnecessary steps and minimises contrast and radiation: brilakis p.489 (28.2.2.1)
- Check 1 (calcium 1400, high likelihood): oxford p.29–30; ESC 2024 calcification and very-high-likelihood statements: ref esc-ccs-2024 (sections above)
- Check 2: ISR accuracy limits oxford p.30; CTCA 10 mSv vs coronary angiogram 6 mSv in Oxford's table: oxford p.37; 10 mSv a historical figure, 2017 median coronary CTA DLP 78% lower than 2007, wide variation by site, scanner and protocol: ref stocker-protection-vi-2018 (abstract)
- Pearl (reading CT saves catheters and runs): this module's reasoning from brilakis p.489 (planning reduces radiation) and oxford p.30 (CT for CTO, anomalies, working views)
- Videos: notes restate titles only (videos.ts entries bpLNDwZ6xi0, _fb4aU6O7CM, Sq2RlA7mp9U)

## Lesson hb-lab-and-radiation-xray-system

- C-arm: X-ray generator on the lower part (under the patient), detector on the upper part (above the patient): oxford p.31–32
- Tube parts: cathode, anode, evacuated glass insert, tube housing, collimators: oxford p.31
- Cathode usually a tungsten coil emitting electrons when heated by current; electrons accelerated by potential difference measured in kV; rotating saucer-shaped tungsten anode disc; X-rays plus heat; rotation dissipates heat; whirring noise from the rotating anode: oxford p.31
- Glass insert within housing with internal lead walls and a small radiolucent window; collimators are lead panels moved in by the radiographer to restrict the beam to the field of interest: oxford p.31
- Beam controlled by kV, mA, filtration (aluminium or copper): oxford p.31
- Table 1.1: kV +number +energy; mA +number =energy; filtration −number +energy: oxford p.32 (checked on the PDF page)
- Higher photon energy reduces absorption and dose but lowers image contrast because more photons reach the detector: oxford p.31
- Integrated dose-rate control modulates mA and kV to keep a constant dose at the detector and steady image quality when projection changes: oxford p.31
- Steep angles: more tissue penetrated, automatic dose increase often not recognised by the operator: brilakis p.491 (28.2.2.4.4)
- Disposable shields in the field of view increase radiation emitted from the tube because less radiation reaches the detector: brilakis p.493 (28.2.2.5.4)
- Image intensifier (input phosphor to light, photocathode to electrons, focused and intensified onto output phosphor, light to camera) and flat panel (phosphor screen to light, then to electrons; these electrons form the digital image; the word 'directly' removed in round 1): oxford p.31–32. Oxford's text says 'electrodes'; the lesson says electrons, as the following sentence of the same paragraph does.
- Both manuals call the detector the image intensifier: oxford p.36–37; brilakis p.489, p.491
- Pulsed fluoroscopy: automated switching of the beam; predefined frames per second; typically 7.5 fps; lower image quality; for scouting, wiring, balloon and stent delivery; storing a few seconds of fluoroscopy: oxford p.33
- Screening uses low-energy X-ray pulses; acquisition uses a higher dose and stores images: oxford p.36. Reading 'low-energy' as low dose per pulse rather than lower photon energy is this module's reasoning from Oxford Table 1.1 (photon energy set by kV, oxford p.32) and oxford p.33 (pulsing lowers exposure)
- Cine: higher dose and frame rate (usually 12.5–30 fps; 7.5 fps at the Oxford Heart Centre); higher resolution and contrast, reduced noise; number and length of runs usually the main source of patient dose; use sparingly: oxford p.33
- Ventriculography frame rates typically 30–60 fps: oxford p.120
- Fluoroscopy 6–7.5 fps, or 3.75 fps in thin patients: brilakis p.491
- Cine about 10× the fluoroscopy dose and not reflected in fluoroscopy time; fluoro save for inflations: brilakis p.490
- Poor image quality causes (large patient, excessive angulation, receptor too far, density differences), prevention (shallower angulation, collimation, filters), treatment (shallower angulation; change frame and pulse rate to increase dose and penetration): brilakis p.125. Brilakis sets no order between the two treatments; 'try the shallower angle first because it costs no extra dose' is this module's reasoning, stated as such in the lesson, tip and case.
- Patient injury from receptor movement, collision sensors; operator injury, move away from moving parts: brilakis p.125–126 (used in tip diag-rad-c-arm-clear)
- Ensure the image intensifier has finished moving before screening: oxford p.36
- Optimisation definition; equipment-based (dose-rate control, shallow projection angles expose less thickness and surface, kV and mA, pulsed fluoroscopy, acquisition) and technique-based (patient position far from generator and close to detector, collimation) strategies: oxford p.33
- Biplane may reduce the number of contrast injections: oxford p.42; biplane ventriculography increases radiation dose and takes time to position: oxford p.119
- Pearl and the "anything in the way makes the tube work harder" framing: this module's reasoning from oxford p.31 (dose-rate control) with brilakis p.491 and p.493 (steep angles; pads in the field)
- Check 2 (18 cine runs, 7 minutes fluoroscopy): brilakis p.490

## Lesson hb-lab-and-radiation-dose

- Gy = J/kg, absorbed dose; equivalent dose weighted by radiation quality factor, Sv; effective dose = dose giving the same risk if received uniformly over the whole body: oxford p.35
- Cumulative air kerma and DAP continuously monitored; dose rate a dynamic parameter: brilakis p.39 (2.5)
- Deterministic effects (skin injury, cataracts) correlate with air kerma to a particular skin area: brilakis p.488
- Collimation reduces skin area exposed and DAP, total air kerma not changed; limits overlap when angles change: brilakis p.491. Round 1: the claim that collimation cuts scatter to the team was removed (not on the cited pages)
- DAP "combines dose with area": from the quantity's name; this module's reasoning
- Dose rate prompts changes if >20 mGy/min: brilakis p.492
- Fluoroscopy time excludes cine: brilakis p.490; fluoroscopy time is in the physiologist's procedure record: oxford p.62; the radiographer records and warns the team about screening time: oxford p.63
- Procedure record includes fluoroscopy time and radiation dose (dose area product): oxford p.62
- Transmitted, scattered and interacting radiation; ionised molecules, free radicals, damage to proteins, enzymes and DNA; effects depend on total energy, type of radiation and energy distribution: oxford p.35
- Deterministic effects: cataracts, impaired fetal development, neurological and gut damage; prolonged radiation to a small skin area from mild erythema to ulceration; stochastic: DNA damage, modified cell that reproduces, cancer: oxford p.35
- Deterministic class defined by a threshold dose and severity rising with dose; stochastic class (cancer and heritable effects) defined by probability, not severity, as a function of dose without threshold: ref icrp-103-2007 (glossary of the free extract on icrp.org). Oxford names the classes without defining them: oxford p.35. Brilakis air-kerma bands (<5 Gy unlikely, 5–10 Gy possible, 10–15 Gy likely): brilakis p.488
- Brilakis: birth defects listed among stochastic complications: brilakis p.488. Oxford: impaired fetal development deterministic: oxford p.35
- Reconciliation: heritable effects stochastic; after prenatal exposure a threshold dose exists for the induction of malformations: ref icrp-103-2007 (executive summary, free extract). Round 2: reading Brilakis's "birth defects" as heritable effects is this module's reasoning and is taught as a conditional interpretation; Brilakis p.488 does not say whether he means heritable effects or malformation after exposure in utero
- Table 1.2 rows (chest X-ray 0.02 mSv, 3 days, 1 in 1,000,000; CT head 2.3 mSv, 1 year, 1 in 10,000; coronary angiogram 6 mSv, 2.7 years, 1 in 3500; CT chest 8 mSv, 3.6 years, 1 in 2500; CT coronary angiogram 10 mSv, 4.5 years, 1 in 2000): oxford p.37 (checked on the PDF page)
- Risk estimates from nuclear bomb survivors and nuclear workers; lifetime cancer risk about one in three: oxford p.37
- Oxford sets an additional risk of fatal cancer against a normal lifetime risk of developing cancer: oxford p.37 (wording of the table header and text)
- Effective dose 'a population measure, not an individual risk': Academy lesson fnd-safety-rad-principles (foundations.ts, dose-quantities table)
- PROTECTION VI paragraph (4502 patients, 61 hospitals, 2017; median DLP 78% lower than 2007; 37-fold variation between hospitals' median DLP): ref stocker-protection-vi-2018 (abstract). 'Do not assume it exceeds that of an invasive study' is this module's reasoning from those figures
- 87% of background from natural sources, mainly radon and gamma rays; medical sources at least 10%: oxford p.35
- IR(ME)R 2017 per Oxford: register of referrers (usually clinicians), practitioners (responsible for accepting requests, usually radiologists), operators (usually radiographers); cardiologist often all three; clinical evaluation recorded for all exposures including fluoroscopy; doses audited; hospitals investigated regularly; greater than expected → local radiation protection adviser; plaintiffs 'could' bring an action under criminal (not civil) law: oxford p.36
- The IR(ME)R statements now cite the Medical Exposure regulations, ref irmer-2017 (SI 2017/1322), in place of irr-2017 (SI 2017/1075, worker protection), which the dose lesson cited in error
- Diagnostic reference levels: employer regularly reviews and makes them available to operators, for interventional procedures where appropriate, and ensures review when consistently exceeded: ref irmer-2017 reg 6(5)(c), 6(7); MPE involved in applying DRLs and in analysing accidental or unintended exposures: reg 14(3)(a), (f). DRL as an indicator of an unusually high dose for a procedure: ref icrp-103-2007 (glossary)
- Exposure significantly greater than generally considered proportionate: immediate preliminary investigation, notification of the enforcing authority, detailed investigation; referrer, practitioner and patient informed of a clinically significant unintended or accidental exposure: ref irmer-2017 reg 8
- Enforced as health and safety regulations under the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, whose offence provisions apply (so a breach is a criminal offence): ref irmer-2017 reg 18
- Women of child-bearing age confirm not pregnant: oxford p.43; radiographer checks and documents pregnancy in line with local policy: oxford p.63; pregnancy test for women of childbearing potential: brilakis p.33
- Radiographer warns at 1 Gy, introduced by Oxford 'as a general rule' (not a figure in the regulations); operator records in report and communicates to patient: oxford p.39
- Lab protocol for announcing the dose (every 1 Gy and/or every 30 min): brilakis p.492
- Incident definition; near miss (could have led to unintended exposure or overexposure, promptly noted to allow preventive action), incident not requiring notification, incident requiring notification; overexposure typically at least ten times intended dose (taught as Oxford's rule of thumb): oxford p.39
- Low frame-rate acquisitions may reduce dose at the expense of image quality; uses include long procedures and pregnancy (used in tip diag-rad-pregnancy-check): oxford p.37
- Follow-up of high-dose cases pointed to cm-patient-complications (brilakis p.493)
- Pearl (cumulative air kerma keeps counting regardless of projection; spreading the dose over several skin areas is less harmful; DAP grows with field size): brilakis p.492 (28.2.2.4.6) and p.491 (collimation and DAP); the statement that cumulative air kerma does not track where the beam pointed is this module's reasoning from brilakis p.492
- Check 3 figures: oxford p.37

## Lesson hb-lab-and-radiation-reduce

- Before (planning, equipment) and during (do not use radiation, minimise dose, shielding and distance for operator only) structure: brilakis p.490 (Fig. 28.4)
- ALARA: oxford p.35–36
- Planning prevents unnecessary steps; studying graft locations on prior angiograms speeds engagement; applies to every procedure; very obese patients on newer X-ray systems: brilakis p.489
- Prior catheterisation or other fluoroscopic procedure, prior images and reports: brilakis p.29 (1.1); radiation skin injury on the back may require postponing or modifying the procedure: brilakis p.33 (1.1); prior skin injury, limit cine runs and keep the area out of the beam: brilakis p.35 (1.6)
- Newer machines: satisfactory images at lower dose; region-of-interest add-on with ≈75% dose reduction: brilakis p.489
- 6–7.5 fps (3.75 in thin patients) adequate in nearly all cases, halves dose compared with 15 fps: brilakis p.491
- Trial: 7.5 vs 15 fps in transradial procedures, both at a fixed 40 nGy per pulse; overall operator dose 30% lower (p<0.0001), patient DAP 19% lower (p=0.022); PCI subgroup DAP 19% lower, not significant (p=0.13); diagnostic subgroup DAP 26% lower (p=0.0018); similar fluoroscopy times: ref abdelaal-low-frame-rate-2014 (abstract, PMID 24746649; Brilakis ch 28 ref 9). 'Halves the fluoroscopy dose' attributes Brilakis's halving to the fluoroscopy dose; the explanation that total DAP fell less because cine is unchanged is this module's reasoning from the trial design
- Low frame-rate acquisitions may reduce dose at the expense of image quality; uses include long procedures and pregnancy: oxford p.37
- Table high, receptor close: brilakis p.491, p.125; image intensifier close to the patient, X-ray source further away reduces skin dose: oxford p.36–37
- Fig. 28.6 values (A: 30 + 80 cm, 110 cm, 1.0; B: 80 cm, 1.4, about 40% higher; C: 110 cm, 2.6, 260%, image 40–50% larger; 3 Gy → 4.2 Gy → 7.8 Gy): brilakis p.492 (checked on the PDF page)
- Fig. 28.6 is reproduced from the 2018 ACC/HRS/NASCI/SCAI/SCCT expert consensus (Brilakis cites the Catheter Cardiovasc Interv version): brilakis p.492; ref radiation-consensus-2018
- Let the image intensifier stop moving before screening: oxford p.36
- Heavy foot defined as using X-ray when it is not needed, with not looking at the screen as the example; lag time in releasing the pedal common early in training: brilakis p.490
- Think before putting the foot on the screening pedal: oxford p.36
- Balloon, wire and stent shaft markers; know the guide length; proximal marker in 90 cm guides may let the device exit before the marker reaches the Y-connector: brilakis p.490
- Trapping minimises X-ray use; fluoroscopy needed only during the initial phase of withdrawal: brilakis p.490, p.160
- Fluoro store instead of cine for inflations; cine ≈10× dose: brilakis p.490
- Radiographer advice when doses high (projection, saving fluoroscopy, collimation, low acquisition rate, avoiding zooming, additional filtration): oxford p.63. Round 1: the 'less scatter' effect removed from the collimation row (not on the cited pages)
- Intravascular imaging limits contrast and radiation: brilakis p.491
- Lower magnification requires less radiation; learning curve; large monitors: brilakis p.491; magnification increases skin dose rate: oxford p.37
- Collimation, especially when increasing field of view; caveat of wire and guide tips out of field (distal wire migration → perforation; deep guide → aortocoronary dissection): brilakis p.491; also brilakis p.40 (2.12)
- Working angle minimised; >30° from AP higher exposure; automatic increase unrecognised; AP includes the spine so slight angulation preferable; RAO less operator exposure, challenging for RCA wiring, excellent for mid-LAD: brilakis p.491
- Reposition image intensifier often; multiple skin areas less deleterious: brilakis p.492; vary the view in prolonged PCI; skin dosimeter on many new systems: oxford p.209
- Dose rate >20 mGy/min; cumulative air kerma monitoring; announce every 1 Gy and/or every 30 min: brilakis p.492; radiographer warns at 1 Gy: oxford p.39
- Stop if air kerma exceeds 5–7 Gy: brilakis p.39; consider stopping at 7–8 Gy and staging: brilakis p.492
- CTO: stop for high radiation dose, usually >5 Gy without lesion crossing or substantial progress: brilakis p.394
- Delayed PCI favoured when excessive radiation during diagnostic procedure or anticipated during PCI, in stable CAD or ACS without active ischaemia; ad hoc PCI needed in most ACS patients with active ischaemia: brilakis p.145 (7.3.1); staged PCI favoured when excessive contrast or radiation in the first part of PCI or anticipated: brilakis p.146 (7.3.2)
- In stable patients with multivessel disease, with high radiation or contrast dose, treating the easier lesions first and deferring the complex ones may be preferable; in ACS with multivessel CAD the culprit lesion(s) first, especially with active ischaemia: brilakis p.146 (7.4). Round 2: the stable-only scope and the ACS culprit-first rule added to step 15
- Check 2 (28 mGy/min, steep LAO cranial, magnification, raised detector): brilakis p.491–492; oxford p.37
- Pearl (say defaults and stopping dose at the brief): this module's reasoning, consistent with brilakis p.492 (lab protocol for dose announcements)

## Lesson hb-lab-and-radiation-operator

- Shielding and distance only reduce operator dose; important given daily exposure over many years: brilakis p.493
- Local radiation protection procedures; Radiation Protection Supervisor checks paperwork and issues dose meters; exposure spanning decades accurately monitored; ALARA: oxford p.35
- Lead glass viewing areas; mobile shields for staff and operator; one-piece aprons or separate top and skirt (latter better for back problems); lead equivalent in mm; most 0.25–0.35 mm LE; higher LE heavier; protection also depends on source output; lead rubber 0.35 mm LE thyroid shields; lead glasses: oxford p.36
- Wear lead protection before the case: oxford p.43
- Radiographer: quality assessment of PPE; warns about screening time, doses, distance from the intensifier, shielding and PPE: oxford p.63
- Fixed shields close to the patient and not too high (Fig. 28.8 suboptimal high and away, optimal low and near): brilakis p.493–494 (figure checked on PDF page 494)
- Shield between patient and operator: brilakis p.125
- Shield and image receptor position continually monitored and adjusted: brilakis p.39
- Disposable pads (over the patient's abdomen) reduce scatter; not in the field of view, which increases tube output: brilakis p.493
- Ceiling-suspended lead: protection and no need to wear lead, avoiding orthopaedic injuries; robotic PCI near elimination of operator dose, few centres; scatter-reducing shield system: brilakis p.489; pads and ceiling-suspended system: brilakis p.595 (30.19)
- Inverse square law; 1 m to 2 m reduces exposure four-fold: oxford p.36; distance, take a step back: brilakis p.493 (Brilakis's wording 'decreases exponentially with distance' is not repeated; the lesson uses the inverse square law as Oxford states it)
- Manifold tubing extensions for the radial operator (Fig. 28.7): brilakis p.493; tubing extension facilitates distance: brilakis p.125
- All appendages, operators' and patients', out of the field: brilakis p.493
- RAO less operator exposure: brilakis p.491
- Concern that radial access may give higher operator dose: oxford p.69
- Real-time operator dose monitors reduce operator dose by about 30%: brilakis p.492; alert the operator: brilakis p.39
- C-arm moving parts can injure the operator: brilakis p.126
- Dose limits: employees 18+ 20 mSv effective, 20 mSv lens, 500 mSv skin and extremities; trainees under 18: 6, 15, 150 mSv: ref irr-2017 (Schedule 3, checked on legislation.gov.uk) and oxford p.39 (Table 1.3)
- Oxford: effective limit averaged over a 5-year period: oxford p.35; IRR 2017 sets 20 mSv per year with an alternative five-year scheme (100 mSv in five years, maximum 50 mSv in any year) in defined circumstances: ref irr-2017 (Schedule 3 Part 2)
- Typical annual dose up to 5 mSv for an interventional cardiologist, 0.5–1 mSv for a radiologist: oxford p.35
- Oxford's 13 mSv abdominal limit for women of reproductive capacity over 3 months: oxford p.39; absent from IRR 2017 Schedule 3: ref irr-2017 (checked)
- Pregnant employee: after written notification to the employer, the equivalent dose to the foetus as low as reasonably practicable and unlikely to exceed 1 mSv for the rest of the pregnancy: ref irr-2017 reg 9(6)(a) and 9(7) (checked on legislation.gov.uk)
- Radiographer duties (ALARP with optimal image quality; records patient radiation and contrast doses per local policy; educates and reassures patients concerned about dose; teaches radiation protection; trains junior radiographers; audits; moves the C-arm to facilitate CPR), within IR(ME)R 2017 and IRR 2017: oxford p.63
