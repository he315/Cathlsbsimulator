## New references (all checked on PubMed: authors, title, journal, year, volume, issue, pages; all figures used are in the PubMed abstract; no full text in PMC for any of them)

- sharp-crossover-tavi-2010: PMID 20049970, Catheter Cardiovasc Interv 2010;75(5):784-93, DOI 10.1002/ccd.22238. Used: crossover technique: opposing femoral artery cannulated with a 7 Fr long sheath; contralateral passage of a balloon and inflation in the proximal iliac; sheath then removed and Prostar sutures tied in a dry field; "balloon optimization of the puncture site was performed as required"; on-table complications (iliac avulsion, two iliac dissections, iliac perforation, common femoral perforation, scrotal haematoma) all repaired by combined surgical and endovascular techniques "using the crossover technique to ensure patient stability". The phrase "over the aortic bifurcation" in the lesson is the anatomical meaning of contralateral passage from the opposite femoral artery into the proximal iliac (module wording, not in the abstract).
- buchanan-modified-crossover-2013: PMID 22511470, Catheter Cardiovasc Interv 2013;81(4):579-83 (PubMed lists the electronic date 2012; volume 81 is 2013), DOI 10.1002/ccd.24380. Used: balloon passed through left radial access and inflated in the proximal iliac to allow percutaneous closure in a clean field; three access-site complications (external iliac rupture, Prostar failure, CFA pseudoaneurysm) treated percutaneously with covered stents via the contralateral femoral artery.
- zaman-cbot-2016: PMID 27107395, Catheter Cardiovasc Interv 2016;88(2):276-84, DOI 10.1002/ccd.26371. Used: CBOT passes a balloon catheter from the contralateral femoral artery to enable controlled closure; routine CBOT n = 55 vs preceding control n = 43 (non-randomised, consecutive patients); CBOT successful in 96%; 30-day access-site major vascular and/or bleeding 5.5% vs 18.6% (P = 0.042).
- stortecky-tavi-vascular-2012: PMID 22625190, JACC Cardiovasc Interv 2012;5(5):515-24, DOI 10.1016/j.jcin.2012.01.021. Cited by Brilakis 29.1.2.4 as reference [3] and Fig. 29.5 (brilakis p.501, reference list p.510). Used: 149 transfemoral TAVI; 27 (18%) vascular complications: incomplete arteriotomy closure 19 (70%), dissection 3, perforation 3, occlusion 1, pseudoaneurysm 1; percutaneous stent graft implantation successful in 21 of 23 (91%); stent patency high at follow-up (median 10.9 months, no haemodynamically significant stenosis); stent fractures in 4 stents (22%), clinically silent; "stent fractures require careful scrutiny". The Swiss location is from the authors' affiliation (Bern).

## Lesson cm-access-complications-femoral-bleeding

- Groin haematoma can also affect the abdominal wall and testicles; femoral bleeding = groin or retroperitoneal haematoma: brilakis p.498
- Local haematoma after femoral access up to 25%: oxford p.77; vascular complications 1–2% of diagnostic catheterisation: oxford p.299; consent figure vascular complications (haematoma, false aneurysm) 3–5% for PCI: oxford p.229
- Higher puncture (above femoral head and inferior epigastric origin) → retroperitoneal bleeding; lower (below femoral head) → pseudoaneurysm and AV fistula: brilakis p.64
- Access-site and distal pulses at beginning and end; continuous inspection and palpation for early identification of haematoma: brilakis p.39 (2.7)
- Groin haematoma diagnosis: groin mass that resolves after manual pressure; challenging/delayed in morbidly obese; US to exclude PSA/AVF; CT location and size: brilakis p.499
- Oxford haematoma: progressively enlarging swelling; extremely painful; occasionally vagal collapse; groin, thigh and abdominal discoloration with time; femoral nerve compression and leg weakness reported; blood count and group and save; higher risk with anticoagulants, thrombolysis, GP IIb/IIIa; firm manual pressure and mechanical compression devices; check distal pulses, listen for bruits (AVF); large haematomas examined to exclude PSA; ultrasonography if uncertain: oxford p.315
- FemoStop-type dome inflated above systolic initially then gradually reduced until haemostasis: oxford p.87 (Fig. 3.9 caption); femoral compression devices used when bleeding hard to control manually: oxford p.87
- Treatment: manual compression proximal to skin entry; painful, analgesics; compressive bandage; bed rest 6–8 h; duplex for ongoing bleeding or PSA; failure → emergency endovascular or surgical treatment: brilakis p.500
- Manual compression bleeding causes (hands too low or to side, inadequate pressure, anticoagulation, hypertension, obesity, haematoma) and treatment (reposition, firm pressure, endovascular or surgery): brilakis p.196–197
- RP causes: high/non-compressible puncture above inferior border of inferior epigastric artery; avoid Angioseal (collagen plug) in high punctures, plug entangled in abdominal wall muscle layers: brilakis p.499; back wall puncture; supratherapeutic/prolonged anticoagulation, GP IIb/IIIa, cangrelor; hypertension; polymer-jacketed wire perforating renal/other peripheral arteries: brilakis p.500
- Oxford: RP with high puncture (above inguinal ligament), can be incessant and fatal; progressive hypotension or collapse; flank bruising occasionally; CT confirms; manual compression unlikely to help; fluid and blood, reversal of anticoagulation, occasionally surgical repair: oxford p.315
- Prevention: fluoroscopic and US guidance and immediate femoral angiography; avoid supratherapeutic anticoagulation; control hypertension; wires through iliacs and aorta under fluoroscopy; never push wires hard against resistance: brilakis p.500
- High puncture: best to remove micropuncture needle and obtain access again: brilakis p.70
- Peripheral perforation causes (forceful wire/catheter into side branch e.g. renal, sheath past wire tip) and prevention (fluoroscopy, redirect wire from side branch): brilakis p.102
- Suspect RP in every patient hypotensive during/after femoral catheterisation: brilakis p.501; signs hypotension, diaphoresis, lower abdominal or back pain; often bradycardia; abdominal/suprapubic tenderness; usually within first 3 hours; closure devices do not exclude; high suspicion in obese, uncontrolled hypertension, high puncture, puncture not guided by US or fluoro, multiple attempts; dented bladder sign if contrast given: brilakis p.499; dented bladder also brilakis p.487 (28.1, hypovolaemia most commonly from bleeding)
- Haemoglobin may be useful but acute bleeding often no immediate fall: brilakis p.501
- Algorithm Fig. 29.4: general measures (large IV or central access; normal saline; type and cross; Hb; reverse anticoagulation "after diagnosis established"); location; in lab: bladder fluoroscopy, contralateral access and femoral angiography; outside: haemodynamically stable? No → (to cath lab path); Yes → CT: RP with active bleeding → active bleeding branch; RP without active bleeding → conservative; no RP → other causes; active bleeding → large vessel → covered stent → fail → surgery; small vessel → coiling → fail → surgery: brilakis p.500 (figure image checked)
- Text of algorithm: central line may be needed for vasopressors and large volumes of crystalloid or blood; several litres of saline may be required; type and cross then transfusion by magnitude of bleeding and haemodynamics; reverse anticoagulation (protamine; discontinue GP IIb/IIIa and cangrelor); manual pressure over access site especially with groin haematoma; dented bladder in lab; femoral angiography shows presence and site; mechanism decides treatment as with coronary perforation; two types: large vessel, small vessel (inferior epigastric or lateral circumflex iliac); balloon inflated at low pressure over site before definitive treatment; covered stent if prolonged inflation fails; may be challenging to deliver; CFA stents prone to fracture: brilakis p.501
- Coil embolisation for small branch; exclude retrograde bleeding; microsphere, cyanoacrylate glue or thrombin for very distal; avoid sealant leakage; prolonged balloon inflation or covered stent across origin of perforated vessel; surgery if covered stent fails or undesirable (e.g. SFA/profunda bifurcation); unstable patients emergently to cath lab or OR; stable → CT; RP without ongoing bleeding conservative; CT shows active extravasation or not: brilakis p.502
- In high suspicion, direct transfer to cath lab may be preferable to non-invasive imaging that delays treatment: brilakis p.207 (11.1.7.8.2.1)
- Bleeding through inferior epigastric/lateral circumflex iliac: embolisation with coils or thrombin: brilakis p.81 (4.2.15.3)

- Tips: access-cx-groin-haematoma-press (p.500, oxford p.315); access-cx-iliac-wire-fluoro (p.500, p.102); access-cx-rp-in-lab (p.500–501, p.487); access-cx-iliofemoral-balloon-first (p.501–502, p.102); access-cx-small-branch-embolise (p.502, p.81)

## Lesson cm-access-complications-femoral-other

- PSA definition (between media and adventitia; true aneurysm all 3 layers); risks infection, rupture, compression neuropathy, DVT; pulsatile tender groin mass, often bruit; duplex flow in and out: brilakis p.502
- Oxford: contained leakage into chamber contained by surrounding tissues; flow in and out through a channel (neck); presentation as for local haematoma; duplex; ultrasound-guided compression or thrombin; surgical repair and endovascular stent closure if these fail: oxford p.315
- Causes: low femoral puncture: brilakis p.502; suboptimal compression; access difficulties such as kinking of wire; artery and vein access; intensive anticoagulation; closure device failure; prevention optimal access and good haemostasis: brilakis p.503
- First-generation closure devices meta-analysis concern, haematoma and possibly PSA: oxford p.89
- Treatment: <2 cm conservative unless severe groin pain, many close spontaneously; ≥2 cm US-guided thrombin, follow-up US 2–3 days; requires narrow neck; wide neck risk of distal thrombin embolisation and acute limb ischaemia, surgery may be preferable; surgery for >6 cm, synthetic grafts, infections, limb ischaemia, skin necrosis; prolonged compression with the US probe (occasionally >30–45 min), operator verifies flow through neck stops, until complete thrombosis; discomfort: brilakis p.503
- Box 10.1: 5000 IU bovine thrombin with 0.9% saline to 5 mL (1000 units/mL); 23-gauge (blue) needle under US; local anaesthesia if extremely tender; small amount may help locate the tip; usual dose 500–1000 units (0.5–1 mL); slow injection, 'smoke' and clot, can be very rapid; reabsorbed over 5–6 weeks; leakage into circulation rare: oxford p.316
- AVF: communication between femoral artery and vein; usually asymptomatic; rarely leg oedema or high-output failure with large shunt: brilakis p.503; bruit and palpable thrill; Doppler US or angiography (simultaneous venous filling, Fig. 29.9); causes: low puncture (vein behind CFA or SFA lower in groin; US shows relation), multiple punctures, artery and vein in same procedure especially same level: brilakis p.504; prevention: arterial sheath first, venous a few minutes later; most no treatment, many close within a year; rarely covered stent if bifurcation not involved, or surgery: brilakis p.505
- Oxford AVF: both punctured with haemorrhage tracking across, or needle and sheath through one vessel into the other; associated with punctures below CFA; management usually surgical: oxford p.315
- Infection: devastating, often surgery and prolonged healing; pain, erythema, discharge; fever, leucocytosis; blood cultures; duplex to exclude PSA; causes haematoma or PSA, prolonged sheath dwell (overnight), immunocompromise (transplant), poor hygiene, closure devices; prevention sterile technique, repeat skin prep before closure device especially after long procedures, avoid closure devices in high infection risk (immunocompromised, skin infection), state-of-the-art access, early sheath removal, prophylactic antibiotics in high-risk, haematomas as culture medium; treatment IV antibiotics, surgery for closure device infection, abscess, prior synthetic graft, persistent bacteraemia: brilakis p.505
- Oxford infection: uncommon, usually Staphylococcus aureus; pain, erythema, discharge, fever; antibiotics according to cultures and rarely abscess drainage: oxford p.316
- Nerve injury: rare, lateral cutaneous or femoral nerve, leg pain; needle or compression by haematoma/PSA: brilakis p.505; prevention access technique; conservative, most resolve, may take several months: brilakis p.506; Oxford femoral nerve compression and leg weakness: oxford p.315
- Video notes: TDcNEUW-qBs, CXdH-QFquHA (titles)
- Tips: access-cx-psa-size-and-neck (p.502–503, oxford p.315); access-cx-arterial-sheath-first (p.505)

## Lesson cm-access-complications-limb

- Manifestations: acute limb ischaemia 6Ps (pain, pallor, paralysis, pulse deficit, paraesthesias, poikilothermia), caused by thrombosis, dissection, embolisation of large thrombi, closure device complications; cholesterol embolisation: microemboli showers, triad leg/foot pain, livedo reticularis, intact pulses; hypereosinophilia and sometimes acute renal failure; in lab angiography; out of lab US or CT: brilakis p.497
- Causes list (dissection, thrombosis, distal embolisation of thrombus/closure components/cholesterol, closure device complications, occlusive sheath, large sheath, prolonged dwell, poor sheath management with aspirate-discard-flush, prolonged or overzealous compression which may also cause DVT, suboptimal anticoagulation) and prevention (meticulous access, fluoroscopic and US guidance avoiding atherosclerosis or calcification, soft deflectable wires, smallest sheath, aspirate-discard): brilakis p.497; avoid pushing catheters against resistance, periodic ACT assessment, radial access: brilakis p.498
- Treatment: contralateral femoral access and angiography; balloon angioplasty, endovascular thrombectomy or thrombolysis; surgical thrombectomy, endarterectomy or bypass; cholesterol embolisation no specific treatment, prevention key, statins may help AKI; Fig. 29.1 Angioseal CFA occlusion removed at emergency surgery: brilakis p.498
- Oxford lower limb: typically associated with closure devices especially with large sheaths; suture device overtightened knot especially multiple devices for larger holes; plug devices new stenosis with pre-existing atherosclerosis or small arteries; prevention: correct puncture (fluoroscopy/US guided), femoral angiogram before removing sheath; below bifurcation or small calibre: manual or mechanical compression; nursing checks pale/cyanotic, cold, loss/weakness of pulses, pain, motility/sensory deficit; prompt diagnosis, early vascular surgery, Doppler US or CT: oxford p.317
- Femoral angiography what can go wrong: dissection causes (aggressive wire advancement, iliofemoral atherosclerosis, injection with catheter tip against wall), prevention (good waveform before injection, avoid forceful injections, 0.035 wire before injection to stabilise sheath, keep tip off wall and maintain true lumen access); treatment non-flow-limiting none; flow-limiting CFA endarterectomy (avoid stent prone to fracture); iliac: contralateral access, true lumen, self-expanding stent; embolisation causes and prevention (US before access, aspiration, separate syringes); air: consider 100% oxygen; thrombus/plaque: endovascular or surgical retrieval if ischaemia: brilakis p.81
- Contrast injection through the micropuncture needle may cause dissection if needle position suboptimal: brilakis p.80
- Retrograde dissections usually sealed by antegrade flow; large may need stenting: brilakis p.102 (5.2.5.1) and p.98
- Sheath aspiration 3–4 mL discarded then flushed with a different syringe: brilakis p.79 (taught as "aspirate first and discard")
- Plug deployed in artery: causes (sheath too far, footplate caught on atherosclerosis; tamping before resistance; arteriotomy in SFA or profunda) and treatment (acute limb ischaemia, prompt, often surgical removal, endovascular described): brilakis p.204
- Larger sheath occupies more of the artery, more limb ischaemia and thrombosis: brilakis p.515 (30.1.1.1)
- Microaxial pump through least diseased femoral artery: brilakis p.259; 14 Fr sheath requires iliofemoral >5 mm without severe calcification, tortuosity or obstruction: brilakis p.251; femoral angiography after sheath insertion to ensure no limb ischaemia: brilakis p.257
- Oxford Impella contraindications include significant peripheral vascular disease; complications include limb ischaemia and vascular trauma: oxford p.295; ECMO contraindications include significant peripheral vascular disease; complications bleeding, limb ischaemia, vascular trauma, compartment syndrome: oxford p.297
- VA-ECMO antegrade perfusion sheath (CFA or SFA), preferably before arterial cannula; NIRS <50% or >15% difference: brilakis p.263
- IABP: ischaemic limb usually recovers after removing IABP and sheath with manual haemostasis, may require surgical or endovascular treatment: brilakis p.257
- Video note: JWh8OaDq6K8 (title)
- Tips: access-cx-cold-leg-six-ps (p.497–498, oxford p.317); access-cx-iliac-dissection-crossover (p.81, p.102); mcs-cx-iabp-ischaemic-leg (p.257, oxford p.317)

## Lesson cm-access-complications-radial-bleeding

- Radial complications list: bleeding (less often than femoral but must be detected early because of compartment syndrome), RAO, spasm, PSA, sterile granuloma, nerve injury: brilakis p.506
- Bleeding at access site or higher in the radial artery; infrequent because artery compressed against wrist with band; local haematoma; uncontrolled forearm bleeding → compartment syndrome needing fasciotomy: brilakis p.506
- Causes: band placement (arterial entry more proximal than skin entry, especially shallow angle; too distal → forearm bleeding; too proximal → wrist haematoma), intensive anticoagulation, wire into side branch, avulsion of side branches by catheter, rupture during aggressive manipulation in spasm, aggressive manipulation especially through loops: brilakis p.506
- Prevention: meticulous technique; close monitoring; band slightly proximal to the puncture; never force wire, sheath or catheter; confirm wire position under X-ray before sheath; avoid large sheaths relative to artery (US); balloon-assisted tracking for loops to avoid razor effect: brilakis p.506
- Access-site bleeding treatment: reposition band; second band; manual pressure and elevation; BP cuff above systolic: brilakis p.506; Oxford local haematoma proximally immediately after band, second device abutting: oxford p.73
- Proximal bleeding: cross and advance catheter → haemostasis in most; crucial to maintain wire position; long peripheral or coronary balloon at low pressure for prolonged time often seals: brilakis p.506–507; compression dressing and ice; fasciotomy for compartment syndrome: brilakis p.507; catheter advancement over perforated segment usually suffices: brilakis p.102
- Fig. 29.10 grades, incidences, definitions, treatments, notes and remarks (BP control, pain management, consider interruption of anticoagulant/antiplatelet infusion, follow forearm and arm diameters, additional bracelets, ice in plastic bag or washcloth, finger O2 saturation during cuff, cuff 20 mmHg below systolic and deflate every 15 minutes, velpeau bandage for a few hours after bracelet removal): brilakis p.507 (image checked)
- Oxford: forearm haematoma up to 1% of radial cases, develops slowly on the ward; conservative: bandage compression, ice, sling elevation; monitor for compartment syndrome; serious bleeding very rare: oxford p.73
- BAT: small compliant balloon sized 1:1 with guide, half in half out, low pressure 3–6 atm; lower pressure more flexible, higher more pushable; prevents razor effect: brilakis p.104 (Fig. 5.9); BAT particularly for radial loops: brilakis p.104
- Oxford upper limb: transient RAO up to 7–8%, mostly silent, ulnar supplies; acute upper limb ischaemia rare, with large uncontrolled forearm haematoma and compartment syndrome; incidence 0.004%–0.13%; causes inadequate haemostasis, undetected radial side-branch perforation (usually wire), laceration during sheath insertion/removal with severe spasm, extravasation from misplaced IV cannula; risk factors female, small size, impaired renal function, large sheath; compartment syndrome without haematoma described, sheath/catheter-induced muscle ischaemia hypothesised; lost equipment (entrapped wire) needing immediate surgery: oxford p.318; prevention: pressure cuff up to 15 mmHg below systolic, pain relief, BP control, heparin reversal; signs: pain (worse on passive stretching), pallor, pulselessness, paralysis, paraesthesia; early vascular surgical involvement: oxford p.319
- Video notes: SU74rGGZTe8, CXdH-QFquHA (titles)

## Lesson cm-access-complications-radial-other

- Oxford: post-procedural loss of radial pulse up to 5%, frequently asymptomatic: oxford p.73; transient occlusion up to 7–8%: oxford p.318
- RAO diagnosis (mostly asymptomatic, rarely pain from acute hand ischaemia, duplex) and causes (small artery or large sheath, non-patent haemostasis, absent or low-intensity anticoagulation, repeat access): brilakis p.507
- Prevention: meticulous access; UFH; compression 2 h or less; patent haemostasis; distal instead of proximal radial; Allen's or Barbeau's test not useful and not routinely needed: brilakis p.508
