## pm-access-site-choice

- Radial (proximal or distal) or ulnar access: significantly fewer access-site complications and greater satisfaction than femoral; engagement can be harder and support suboptimal (respiratory motion, subclavian tortuosity, large guides not possible) — brilakis p.61
- Brachial, transcaval, carotid rarely used except structural; expertise in both radial and femoral essential — brilakis p.61. Oxford: knowledge of the landmarks and complications of whichever approach is used is paramount (no competence statement) — oxford p.67
- 2018 ESC quote: radial preferred for any PCI irrespective of presentation unless overriding procedural considerations — brilakis p.61; ref esc-revasc-2018
- 2023 ESC ACS guideline: radial access recommended as standard approach (class I) unless overriding procedural considerations — ref esc-acs-2023 (recommendation in the section on technical aspects of invasive strategy)
- Radial replaced femoral over last decade; commonest in UK, Europe, Asia; driven by randomised and registry data showing less bleeding and lower mortality; mortality benefit thought (not proven) to come from less bleeding — oxford p.69
- Radial benefits: less access-site bleeding, lower mortality, quicker mobilisation, patient comfort and satisfaction, lower healthcare costs — oxford p.69
- Radial drawbacks: steep learning curve with longer procedures and failures for femorally trained operators; possible higher operator radiation; occlusion (relevant for future dialysis or total arterial grafting); uncomfortable arm position with arm/shoulder injury — oxford p.69
- Femoral: severely diseased or occluded iliofemoral (or subclavian) arteries should be avoided — brilakis p.34
- Femoral mobilisation after 3 hours of bed rest — oxford p.76
- Femoral route for balloon pump, microaxial pump and VA-ECMO insertion — brilakis p.251, p.257, p.263 (14.3–14.5)
- Five-step algorithm (Fig 4.1) with step order, step 1 exclusions (radial harvesting, occluded femoral/radial, CT occlusion or severe disease of CFA/iliac/distal aorta, extreme iliac or subclavian tortuosity, failure to engage previously, dialysis shunt, strong preference: music players and walker users avoid radial; unable to lie flat, back pain or heart failure, prefer radial) — brilakis p.61, p.62 (figure)
- Step 2: prior CABG with patent grafts → femoral usually preferred (harder graft engagement radially); if radial, left radial for LIMA; bilateral IMA → femoral strongly favoured — brilakis p.61
- Step 3: lower mortality with radial in STEMI in several but not all studies, likely from aggressive antithrombotics; OAC or high bleeding risk (high or low BMI, renal failure, anaemia, thrombocytopenia) → radial — brilakis p.61
- High INR and low platelets favour radial; morbid obesity favours radial; arteria lusoria favours left radial or femoral; CT shows anomalous arch, iliac/subclavian size and disease, CFA bifurcation; desired outcome includes sheath size and length — brilakis p.34
- Step 4: complex PCI (CTO, severe calcification and tortuosity, challenging bifurcations) → at least one femoral, often 7 or 8 Fr, ideally 45 cm; better support, may improve efficiency, success, safety — brilakis p.61
- Step 5: change site on failure (wire/catheter to root, engagement, PCI); radial-to-femoral crossover in anticoagulated patients → more bleeding, extreme care, meticulous technique — brilakis p.61
- Oxford situations table: CABG (femoral or left radial with LIMA; LIMA from right radial possible but challenging with ostial injury risk; vein grafts awkward from right radial due to brachiocephalic entry; RIMA may only be selectively engaged from right radial) — oxford p.67
- PVD: not a contraindication to femoral; if neither femoral pulse palpable (or poorly and cannot be accessed) → radial; axillofemoral graft → aorta may be occluded, femoral pointless; puncturing Dacron grafts generally not recommended (bleeding) — oxford p.67
- AAA: suspect if wire/catheter loops back or deviates laterally; exchange over a wire; no absolute size threshold, weigh risk/benefit; thrombus on ultrasound contraindicates femoral (renal failure, distal ischaemia, trash foot) — oxford p.68
- Coarctation: usually just distal to left subclavian; may not cross from femoral; radial, or femoral + radial for gradient — oxford p.68
- Anticoagulated: radial; femoral reserved for emergencies such as IABP — oxford p.68; femoral generally safer if INR < 2.0 on warfarin; consider fluoroscopy and ultrasound for femoral puncture in anticoagulated — oxford p.85
- 1 Fr = 0.33 mm; 6 Fr sheath accepts 2.0 mm catheter; 6 Fr sheath OD 2.62 mm ≈ 8 Fr guide 2.67 mm — brilakis p.515
- Diagnostic 4–6 Fr (4 Fr often needs automated injector); PCI 5–8 Fr; complex 7–8 Fr; IABP 7–9 Fr (also sheathless); microaxial 13 Fr (2.5) and 14 Fr (CP); ECMO 15–19 Fr arterial, 25 Fr venous — brilakis p.515
- Larger sheaths occupy more of the artery → limb ischaemia, thrombosis — brilakis p.515
- Length 10 cm usual; 25 or 45 cm for obesity, iliac/aortic tortuosity, support; tips reach internal iliac origin, aortic bifurcation, diaphragm — brilakis p.515–516
- Radial 5 or 6 Fr most common; 7 or 8 Fr often in larger radial arteries — brilakis p.516; Oxford radial sheaths generally 5 or 6 Fr — oxford p.71
- Oxford femoral: 6 Fr typical; 4 or 5 Fr diagnostic; 7 or 8 Fr complex intervention — oxford p.75
- Biradial and radial CTO PCI increasingly used — brilakis p.390 (21.4)
- Pitfall "wire loops or swings laterally in abdomen" — oxford p.68

- 2024 ESC CCS guideline: 'When ICA is indicated, radial artery access is recommended as the preferred access site', class I, level A (Recommendation Table 11, section 3) — ref esc-ccs-2024 (checked against the guideline PDF text, doi 10.1093/eurheartj/ehae177)
- Checklist 1.4: Angioseal within 90 days → consider contralateral femoral or radial (advice to consider, Angioseal named; not an exclusion); prior access complication → avoid that site; review prior catheterisation films for aortoiliac and upper-extremity disease or tortuosity; consider peripheral artery ultrasound; hands/arms for work or support may prefer femoral — brilakis p.33–34
- Oxford anticoagulation: compression or closure devices may attenuate bleeding after femoral procedures; guidelines only, local protocols apply — oxford p.85
- Longer sheaths give better guide support and torque response; long sheath 1 Fr larger than the catheter for very tortuous iliacs — brilakis p.515
- Fig 30.2: share of a 6 mm femoral lumen: 6 Fr 14.5%, 8 Fr 30.6%, 11 Fr 64.8% — brilakis p.516
- Table 30.2 thin-walled vs standard OD: 5 Fr 2.14 vs 2.29, 6 Fr 2.44 vs 2.62, 7 Fr 2.79 vs 2.95 mm; easier insertion, less spasm, more kinking — brilakis p.517
- Sheathless options: 7.5 Fr sheathless guide through a 5 Fr-equivalent puncture; standard 7 or 8 Fr guide through short 7 or 8 Fr sheath; Railway dilator through the guide; 125 cm diagnostic catheter as dilator for a 90 or 100 cm guide 2 Fr larger; balloon-assisted tracking — brilakis p.517
## pm-access-seldinger

- Seldinger, Swedish radiologist, 1953, originally for arteriography; modified for veins, arteries, hollow organs (chest drains, enterogastrostomy tubes) — oxford p.65; ref seldinger-technique-1953
- Sequence: bevelled hollow needle; atraumatic curved-tip wire; needle removed; introducer sheath with or without dilator; wire removed; haemostatic valve — oxford p.66
- Brilakis keeps the 0.035 wire after removing the dilator (until femoral angiogram) — brilakis p.77 (4.2.13.2 step 3), p.79 (4.2.15.2 step 3)
- Micropuncture 21 G (OD 0.82 mm) vs 18 G (1.27 mm); not proven superior; likely less arterial injury; allows reattempt without significant bleeding; 0.018 wire more likely to enter side branches (inferior epigastric, lateral circumflex iliac) and perforate than 0.035 — brilakis p.62
- Micropuncture flow less evident, ideally pulsatile; 18 G → straight to 0.035 wire — brilakis p.70
- Micropuncture dilator, remove inner and 0.018 wire, brisk pulsatile flow, 0.035/0.038 wire — brilakis p.74, p.76
- Radial sheath package contains needle or Angiocath and 0.018 wire; sheath over 0.018 wire — brilakis p.86, p.90
- Polymer-jacketed wire never through a needle (coating sheared on retraction) — brilakis p.98
- UNIVERSAL: 621 patients, fluoroscopic landmarking background; composite BARC 2/3/5 bleeding or major vascular complications 12.9% vs 16.1% (P = .25); first-pass 86.6% vs 70.0%; venipuncture 3.1% vs 11.7% — ref jolly-universal-2022 (PubMed abstract)
- UNIVERSAL closure-device subgroup: composite 11.8% vs 23.4% with a closure device; no difference without (interaction p = 0.004) — ref dentremont-universal-closure-2023 (PubMed abstract)
- Pooled FAUST/UNIVERSAL (1624 patients from both trials): operators with >20 ultrasound-guided cases had fewer complications than non-ultrasound access (5/240 vs 64/810) — ref dentremont-ultrasound-learning-2024 (PubMed abstract, PMID 38887948). Seldinger keypoint attributes the >20 result to the pooled data, not to UNIVERSAL alone.
- Ultrasound strongly recommended for arterial and venous access; safety, efficiency, operator expertise; weak pulses, PAD, obesity, failed palpation, both femoral artery and vein — brilakis p.66
- FAUST numbers (1004 patients; CFA cannulation 86.4 vs 83.3%; high bifurcation subgroup; first pass 83 vs 46%; attempts 1.3 vs 3.0; venipuncture 2.4 vs 15.8%; complications 1.4 vs 3.4%) — ref seto-faust-2010 (PubMed abstract)
- RAUST numbers (698 patients; attempts 1.65 vs 3.05; first pass 64.8 vs 43.9%; time reduced; no difference in spasm, pain or bleeding) — ref seto-raust-2015 (PubMed abstract)
- Probe: gel inside and outside sterile cover — brilakis p.66; saline at wrist — brilakis p.84
- Artery lateral to vein, not compressible; colour Doppler; depth and gain — brilakis p.67
- Probe perpendicular; bevel up — brilakis p.69
- Needle not visible: change entry, change probe location/angle, echogenic needles — brilakis p.70, p.86
- No blood: outside or along wall, or plugged → reposition, remove and flush; dark non-pulsatile = vein → partially withdraw and redirect laterally — brilakis p.70
- Never push wire against resistance — brilakis p.70, p.81, p.98
- Aspirate 3–4 mL, discard, flush with a different syringe of heparinised saline — brilakis p.79; separate syringes so debris is not reinjected — brilakis p.81
- Connect to manifold, waveform without damping — brilakis p.79
- Oxford flushes femoral arterial sidearm (heparinised saline) and venous sheath sidearm — oxford p.75, p.80

## pm-access-femoral-puncture

- Target over femoral head (bone for compression); high (above femoral head and above inferior epigastric origin) → retroperitoneal bleeding; low (below femoral head) → pseudoaneurysm, AV fistula — brilakis p.64
- Oxford: CFA above bifurcation, below inguinal ligament; radiographically at femoral head ~0.5–3 cm below ligament; fluoroscopy especially in overweight; high puncture above ligament → retroperitoneal — oxford p.75, p.77
- High micropuncture → remove and redo; low → may be preferable to redo — brilakis p.70
- Vein often behind artery lower in groin — brilakis p.504 (29.1.4.2)
- Palpation hand positions (right: left thumb ASIS, middle finger symphysis, index over CFA; left reversed) — brilakis p.62–63
- Oxford: leg abducted, foot turned out; palpate at or above groin crease — oxford p.75
- Weak/absent pulse → ultrasound for patency and size — brilakis p.63
- Prep: both groins ideally, shave, antiseptic centre to periphery, dry, drape; pannus taping or retention system; contamination → re-scrub; drape misplaced → reposition after prepping — brilakis p.64
- Fluoroscopy: haemostat over pulse, AP, tip at inferior border of femoral head, mark with sterile marker or micropuncture needle; obese → push tissue up from crease; hands out of beam; fluoroscopy cannot reliably show bifurcation → ultrasound — brilakis p.64–65
- Ultrasound: probe above the marked line, scan up and down for bifurcation and calcification; choose above bifurcation, away from calcium, intraluminal disease, bypass grafts, recent closure devices; cannot identify (obesity, prior surgery, drape, inexperience, CFA disease) → colour Doppler, re-examine X-ray landmarks, reposition drape, alternative site, depth/gain; whole CFA calcified → alternative site — brilakis p.66–67
- LA: 10 mL syringe, 26–31 G needle, aspirate, 10–20 mL subcutaneous; leave needle in skin when exchanging syringes; ultrasound to visualise; adjust angle if not seen; other hand away (needle injury); vessel punctured → reposition; much blood → discard and new syringe — brilakis p.69
- Oxford: 10 mL 1% lidocaine; incise skin and open superficial layers with forceps; 30–45° bevel up until arterial blood pulses freely — oxford p.75
- Puncture: visualise target and needle, adjust entry and angle, probe perpendicular, bevel up — brilakis p.69
- Depth figure: 2 cm deep at 45°; 4 cm deep: move entry caudally (option 1, preferred) or steeper 70° (option 2); shallower angle → entry farther from probe — brilakis p.70 (Fig 4.13 caption), p.71 (Fig 4.14)
- Needle not visible, no blood, vein → fixes as above — brilakis p.70

- Deep artery geometry (check stem and q4): at 45° from the usual skin point a 4 cm-deep artery is met further cranially than a 2 cm one, hence the preferred caudal move of the entry point — brilakis p.71 (Fig 4.14), p.70 (Fig 4.13 caption)
- Skin line at inferior border is a reference; wire entry must be within the lower half of the femoral head — brilakis p.63 (Fig 4.6), p.72 (4.2.8.2)
- Oxford X-ray target: femoral head, about 0.5–3 cm below the inguinal ligament; fluoroscopy before puncture especially in overweight; Fig 3.5: ideal site between skin crease and inguinal ligament; local anaesthetic without puncturing vein or artery — oxford p.75
- Needle not visible: Brilakis gives solutions only, no cause (table cell says 'Not given by Brilakis') — brilakis p.70
- Retroperitoneal haematoma can be challenging to diagnose, usually develops in first 3 hours, not excluded by closure devices — brilakis p.498 (29.1.2)
- Palpation landmark is the anterior superior iliac spine — brilakis p.62–63
## pm-access-femoral-sheath

- 0.018 wire 40 cm in the Cook kit (module says "one common kit"); fluoroscopy; side branches lateral circumflex iliac, inferior epigastric, internal iliac; do not push against resistance — brilakis p.70
- Resistance causes and solutions (needle position, inferior direction/small branch, disease/tortuosity, low puncture; reposition for backflow, redirect caudal wire, continue along anticipated tortuous course) — brilakis p.70–71
- Wire screen: entry within lower half of femoral head; anticipated course; remove needle with pressure; too high/low → remove, pressure 1–2 min, re-puncture under US; medial = inferior epigastric, lateral = lateral circumflex iliac, inferior → reposition — brilakis p.72–73
- Skin nick optional; over needle; or after micropuncture dilator; never with sheath in place (cut sheath, bleeding); not too deep; reasonable size; non-working hand away; helps Perclose knot pusher — brilakis p.73–74
- Micropuncture dilator over 0.018; remove inner and wire; brisk pulsatile flow; 0.035/0.038 to abdominal aorta; long 180 cm wire; stiff wire for scarred groins; failure causes (dilator kink, tortuosity, side branch, subintimal) → fluoroscopy — brilakis p.74, p.76–77
- Sheath: 10 cm most; 25 or 45 cm for iliac tortuosity, iliac stents, obese (short may not reach), extra back-up; advance to hub; remove dilator leaving wire — brilakis p.77
- Aspirate 3–4 mL, discard; flush with different syringe — brilakis p.79
- Angiogram: manifold, waveform without damping, wire into aorta if not in place, centre sheath, low magnification, 20–30° RAO right / 20–30° LAO left, cine or fluoro-store, 5–10 mL, diluted contrast allowed, sheath pulled medially; assess perforation, dissection/filling defect, antegrade flow, disease/calcium/tortuosity, puncture location/size/quality for closure — brilakis p.79–80
- Contrast through micropuncture needle: advantage and disadvantage — brilakis p.80
- Fig 4.29: high bifurcation, sheath in small accessory profunda, no closure device, manual compression — brilakis p.80
- Complications table (perforation causes/prevention/treatment incl. coils or thrombin, upsizing sheath, covered stent, surgery, no anticoagulation and no PCI if nonemergent; dissection causes/prevention incl. wire before injection which stabilises sheath, keeps tip off wall and keeps true-lumen access; treatment non-flow-limiting none, CFA endarterectomy to avoid stent fracture, iliac contralateral access with self-expanding stent; embolism causes/prevention/treatment incl. 100% oxygen for air, retrieval if limb ischaemia) — brilakis p.81
- Oxford complications: haematoma up to 25%, vasovagal at puncture or removal, tear/dissection uncommon unless diseased/calcified, retroperitoneal after high puncture, pseudoaneurysm, distal embolisation from sheath — oxford p.77

- Ipsilateral oblique facilitates visualisation of the bifurcation (pitfall reworded; no claim about the contralateral view) — brilakis p.80
- Tip access-femoral-repuncture-wrong-height: entry not within the lower half of the femoral head → remove, press 1–2 min, re-puncture — brilakis p.72
## pm-access-radial-puncture

- Palpate proximal to styloid — brilakis p.82; Oxford three fingers proximal to flexor retinaculum — oxford p.71
- Allen/Barbeau no longer recommended; RADAR — brilakis p.82; ref valgimigli-radar-allen-2014 (203 patients across Allen results, no hand ischaemic complications — PubMed abstract)
- Allen/Barbeau not useful for predicting RAO — brilakis p.508 (29.2.2.3)
- Oxford Allen test details (6–7 fist cycles, release ulnar, 10 s, oximeter, say "normal"); some operators require normal test — oxford p.73
- Right radial positioning (dedicated devices or alongside) — brilakis p.82; Oxford arm extended, supine, relaxed, wrist hyperextended, strap to board, roll under wrist/elbow — oxford p.71
- Left radial: less subclavian tortuosity, slightly shorter fluoroscopy; more challenging, less comfortable; patent LIMA; elbow support devices or blankets/pillows over abdomen in supination; finger traps partial supination; long 16–25 cm sheath partially inserted; sheath extension device, operator returns to right side — brilakis p.82–83
- Distal left radial positioning: thumb grasped under four fingers, hand slightly abducted — brilakis p.83
- Shave; scrub both radial and ulnar sites; dry; drape — brilakis p.83
- Oxford: shave/paint forearm; some prep right femoral (radial difficulty, IABP); avoid IV cannulae same arm; remove bracelets, watches — oxford p.71
- US: reduces attempts → less spasm; segment above styloid; probe in sterile bag; above styloid or snuffbox; gel or saline; low depth, smaller and more superficial — brilakis p.84; RAUST numbers — ref seto-raust-2015
- Cannot identify: obesity, prior harvest, inexperience, occlusion → colour Doppler, alternative sites (ipsilateral ulnar, contralateral radial/ulnar, femoral); small artery → alternative site — brilakis p.84
- LA: 3 mL syringe, 31 G, aspirate, ~1 mL; larger compress; US; spasm → nitroglycerin sublingual or periradial mixed 1:1 with 1% lidocaine; too much → wait 1–2 min or different location; intra-arterial lidocaine → seizures; operator needle injury — brilakis p.85
- Oxford 1–2 mL 1% lidocaine subcutaneously, not puncturing artery — oxford p.71
- Puncture ~1 cm proximal to radial styloid, along course, 30–60° (Fig 3.2) — oxford p.71–72
- Micropuncture bevel up; single wall vs through-and-through; Angiocath technique — brilakis p.86
- Needle not visible fixes; failure to puncture causes (small, spasm, proximal occlusion with palmar arch collateral pulse) — brilakis p.86
- Spasm risk factors (small, younger, multiple passes, IV vasopressors or cardiac arrest), prevention (avoid very small → ulnar; US; moderate sedation; SL or SC nitroglycerin), treatment (vasodilators SL nitroglycerin, enhanced sedation; switch to ipsilateral ulnar, contralateral radial, femoral) — brilakis p.87
- Additional causes: women, smokers, anxiety, large sheaths relative to artery, no vasodilators — brilakis p.508 (29.2.3.2)
- Oxford spasm prevented by mild sedation (opiates, benzodiazepines) — oxford p.73
- No blood return fixes — brilakis p.87

- RADAR caveats: 203 patients recruited from 942 screened (83 normal, 60 intermediate, 60 abnormal Allen), grouped by Allen result (not randomised), elective patients (registered title), primary endpoint thumb capillary lactate, authors call it proof of concept needing broader validation — ref valgimigli-radar-allen-2014 (PubMed abstract)
- Ultrasound reduces attempts, which 'can reduce' spasm (Brilakis's argument) — brilakis p.84, p.508; RAUST found no significant difference in spasm — ref seto-raust-2015
- Spasm prevention list (meticulous technique, ultrasound, vasodilator, thin small sheath, avoid very small radials, avoid excess manipulation, moderate sedation, adequate local anaesthesia, long 24 cm sheaths) — brilakis p.508–509 (29.2.3.3); pearl reworded as prevention first
- Oxford nicks skin superficially over intended site before puncture; sheath inserted gently — oxford p.71
## pm-access-radial-sheath

- Wire: single wall through needle; through-and-through withdraw until flow — brilakis p.87–88
- Resistance causes (needle position, tortuosity, side branch, spasm); never push hard (dissection, spasm, perforation); backflow; wrist extension; angle change; rotate bevel; higher in forearm → fluoroscopy; diagnostic catheter to wire tip and radial angiography — brilakis p.88, p.90
- Fig 4.40 loop example (spasm on 0.035, coronary wire, sheath over coronary wire, pseudolesions, 5 Fr Judkins diagnostic, PCI crossover to femoral) — brilakis p.90
- Nick: remove needle; scalpel at wire entry; not deep; longitudinal not sagittal; other hand away — brilakis p.90
- Sheath size by artery (US) and procedure; 4 Fr diagnostic or thin-walled preferred (lower profile, less spasm); long sheaths usually avoided (RAO and spasm); dip in saline; advance to hub; remove dilator and wire; left radial: distal radial or partial long sheath — brilakis p.90–91
- Thin-walled 6 Fr OD 2.44 vs standard 2.62 mm; thin walls may kink more — brilakis p.517 (Table 30.2)
- Oxford radial sheaths 5 or 6 Fr, short or long, sometimes hydrophilic — oxford p.71
- Sheath won't advance: skin → scalpel; spasm → sedation, IA verapamil or nitroglycerin, SC nitroglycerin; small → ulnar, contralateral radial, femoral — brilakis p.91
- Aspirate 3–4 mL — brilakis p.91; vasodilators verapamil 2–3 mg, nicardipine 100–200 mcg, nitroglycerin 100–200 mcg; before sheath removal may reduce RAO — brilakis p.92
- Engagement chapter: verapamil 2.5 mg and/or nitroglycerin 100–200 mcg after sheath insertion — brilakis p.106
- Heparin 50 U/kg up to 5000 U diagnostic; 100 U/kg may be more effective; may delay until catheter at root with extreme tortuosity (crossover) — brilakis p.92
- Oxford cocktail heparin 2500 IU, verapamil 2.5 mg, GTN 200–400 micrograms; regimens vary — oxford p.71
- Securing: haemostat clip to drape, Tegaderm-type cover, suture, sheath extension clasp — brilakis p.93
- Oxford complications: spasm common, prevented by mild sedation, IA nitrates and verapamil; pulse loss up to 5% often asymptomatic; local haematoma proximal to device → second device abutting; forearm haematoma up to 1%, bandage, ice, sling, monitor for compartment syndrome; serious bleeding very rare — oxford p.73

- Heparin delay with extreme radial or subclavian tortuosity (check stem now says extreme subclavian tortuosity on the previous report) — brilakis p.92
- Long (24 cm) radial sheaths reaching the brachial artery to exclude the radial from catheter manipulation and limit spasm — brilakis p.509 (29.2.3.3)
- Nitroglycerin not given in hypotension, RV infarction, HOCM or after recent PDE-5 inhibitor — brilakis p.42 (3.2.1.3.1); verapamil given for radial access listed as a cause of hypotension — brilakis p.124 (6.3.3.1). Severe aortic stenosis and bradycardia cautions were not added: not in either manual
- Forearm haematoma up to 1%, develops slowly on the ward (reworded) — oxford p.73
## pm-access-alternative

- Distal radial technique: thumb grasp, hand abducted; probe over snuffbox; 30–45° lateral to medial toward strongest pulse proximal in snuffbox — brilakis p.83, p.84, p.86
- Oxford snuffbox description (dorsal course, palpable over bony snuffbox between thumb and index finger); pronate arm after sheath; benefits list (easier left radial, limited supination, possible easier retrograde recanalisation with lower occlusion risk, lower compartment syndrome, shorter haemostasis) — oxford p.74
- Table 4.1 entries as summarised (checked against the PDF image) — brilakis p.84
- Distal radial disadvantages: smaller artery limits sheath, longer access — brilakis p.83
- Failure to reach root: very tall, severe subclavian/iliac/aortic tortuosity, distal radial; prevention: long sheaths, stiff wire, longer (125 cm) catheters — brilakis p.106 (5.3.4)
- Distal radial or long radial sheaths for left radial CTO PCI — brilakis p.390 (21.4)
- Distal radial among RAO prevention measures — brilakis p.508 (29.2.2.3)
- DISCO RADIAL: 1307 patients (657 + 650), 6 Fr Slender sheath, forearm RAO 0.91% vs 0.31% (P = 0.29), crossover 3.5% vs 7.4%, haemostasis median 180 vs 153 min, spasm 2.7% vs 5.4% — ref aminian-disco-radial-2022 (PubMed abstract)
- Ulnar: similar to radial, deeper, often below flexor carpi ulnaris tendon, close to ulnar nerve (painful) — brilakis p.93; ulnar in place of very small radial — brilakis p.87; wrist route for CTO — brilakis p.390
- Brachial: infrequent; hard haemostasis (less compressible, no bands); higher nerve injury risk with haematoma; antecubital with ultrasound — brilakis p.93; brachial or distal radial for recanalising occluded radial in symptomatic patients — brilakis p.508 (29.2.2.4)
- Ulnar and brachial rarely used — brilakis p.515 (30.1)
- Axillary: sometimes for support devices; beyond scope — brilakis p.93; axillary/transcaval need large-bore expertise — brilakis p.251 (14.1.5)
- Oxford: Impella via femoral or axillary — oxford p.295; TAVI: CT of femoral and axillary — oxford p.30 (ch 1: Cardiac CT or invasive angiography?); angiography of axillary and subclavian when iliofemoral disease — oxford p.131 (ch 5 TAVI work-up)
- Transcaval: support devices and TAVR; beyond scope — brilakis p.94; arterial cannula placement in severe PAD (Table 14.1 footnote b, applies to microaxial pump, TandemHeart and VA-ECMO columns) — brilakis p.254
- Carotid rarely, structural — brilakis p.61
- Dacron grafts generally not recommended — oxford p.67

- Table 4.1 'Time to hemostasis' is a single '?' spanning both columns (now in the no-difference column as uncertain) — brilakis p.84 (image checked)
- Longer catheters: balloons and stents may not reach the lesion if PCI needed; longest DES shafts 145, 144, 140 cm; other solutions guide extensions, parallel sheath, change access site (femoral usually shorter) — brilakis p.106 (5.3.4)
- TENDERA: 850 patients, 5 or 6 Fr hydrophilic sheaths; 12-month forearm RAO 2.5% vs 6.7%; crossover 4.6% vs 1%; shorter haemostasis (156.5 vs 180 min) — ref babunashvili-tendera-2024 (PubMed abstract)
- RAPID: 439 patients without PCI analysed; crossover 14.9% vs 8.3%; RAO 20.3% vs 21.2% (p = 0.810) — ref stiermaier-rapid-2025 (PubMed abstract)
- Meta-analysis 2026: 20 RCTs, 8718 patients; RAO OR 0.26; crossover OR 2.28; procedural success lower (OR 0.53); no significant difference in spasm or time to haemostasis — ref singh-distal-radial-meta-2026 (PubMed abstract)
- Transcaval: Oxford has no mention of transcaval access (grep of all Oxford chapters); Brilakis beyond scope — brilakis p.94
- Brachial check stem: reasons are Brilakis's (difficult haemostasis, no band, nerve injury with haematoma) — brilakis p.93
## pm-access-venous

- Indications (RH pressures/saturations, CO, shunt, pulmonary angiography, biopsy); route depends on procedure, patient, operator — oxford p.79
- Femoral vein: medial; NAVY; shave/paint; 5–10 mL 1% lidocaine; protect artery laterally with left hand; needle on heparinised saline syringe, gentle negative pressure; dark blood flows freely → disconnect, wire; skin incision before or after; 5 or 6 Fr; flush sidearm; indications (routine left and right heart cath; temporary pacing and central lines in anticoagulated/clotting abnormality, low complication risk); complications (arterial puncture, haematoma, infection if line not removed) — oxford p.80
- Right heart usually from right femoral vein; catheter can lodge in venous side branches → withdraw and rotate under screening — oxford p.111
- Temporary pacing: usually femoral vein, sheath larger than the wire — oxford p.277
- PE: femoral venous access may dislodge iliofemoral clot — oxford p.139
